Foundations: Islam and communities

The region's religious communities, and the course's central warning: religion does not explain politics by itself. Politics turns religious difference into political difference. Keep that idea ready, because it is the alternative theory you will rebut in almost any essay.

Sunni / Shi'i

Thread: religion and politics
What happened

The split began as a dispute over who should lead the Muslim community after Muhammad died in 632. Sunnis accepted the caliphs chosen by the community, beginning with Abu Bakr. The Shi'at Ali, the "party of Ali," held that leadership belonged to Ali, Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law, and to his descendants. The killing of Ali's son Husayn at Karbala in 680 became the defining story of Shi'i martyrdom. Most Shi'is today are Twelvers, who follow a line of twelve imams, the last of whom is believed to be hidden and expected to return.

Why it mattered

For centuries the difference was mainly religious. It became a political boundary when a state adopted it. In 1501 the Safavid dynasty made Twelver Shi'ism the state religion of Iran and brought in Shi'i scholars to build it. The Ottomans were Sunni, so their wars with the Safavids, such as Chaldiran in 1514, took on a religious edge. That is a large part of why Iran is majority Shi'i while the former Ottoman lands around it are mostly Sunni.

Actors

Safavid shahs, Ottoman sultans, and the Shi'i ulama the Safavids imported.

Connections

Safavid and Qajar empires (M2), ulama (this module), Iranian constitutionalism (M3), Hamas and the wider politics of the 1980s (M6).

Interpretations
  • Primordialist: an ancient hatred that drives conflict. Weak, because Sunnis and Shi'is lived side by side and intermarried for long periods without conflict, and because it cannot explain timing.
  • Instrumentalist and constructivist: states and elites turn the difference into conflict when it serves them. The Safavid decision of 1501 is the classic case. This is the view the course material supports.
Today

outside context The Saudi and Iranian rivalry after 1979 is often called sectarian, yet Iran has supported the Sunni group Hamas. That pattern suggests geopolitics uses sect more than sect drives geopolitics.

Model ID

Sunni and Shi'i Islam divided over who should lead the Muslim community after Muhammad's death in 632: Sunnis accepted caliphs chosen by the community, while Shi'is held that leadership belonged to Ali and his descendants. Shi'i identity was shaped by the martyrdom of Husayn at Karbala in 680, and most Shi'is today are Twelvers. The difference became a political boundary when the Safavids made Twelver Shi'ism Iran's state religion after 1501, setting a Shi'i empire against the Sunni Ottomans. This is why Iran is majority Shi'i while most of its former Ottoman neighbors are Sunni. The divide matters politically when states and elites mobilize it, not because of a constant ancient hatred.

Druze

Thread: religion and politics
What happened

A religious community that grew out of Ismaili Shi'ism in 11th century Fatimid Egypt, around the caliph al-Hakim. It is a closed community: it accepts no converts, and its full doctrine is reserved for initiates. Druze live mainly in Lebanon, southern Syria, and northern Israel.

Why it mattered

In Mount Lebanon, Druze lords such as the Jumblatt family were the dominant landholders, and many Maronite peasants worked their land. In the 19th century that balance shifted. Maronites grew in number and wealth, and Ottoman reform and European involvement changed how the mountain was governed. Fighting broke out in the 1840s and peaked in 1860.

Actors

Druze notable families, Maronite peasants and clergy, Ottoman officials, and France and Britain as outside patrons.

Connections

Maronite and sectarianism (this module), Tanzimat and the Eastern Question (M3), the French mandate (M5).

Interpretations

One reading treats 1860 as a religious war. The stronger reading treats it as a conflict over land, status, and political order, in which religious labels became the language of a struggle that Ottoman reform and European patronage had reshaped.

Today

The Jumblatt-led party remains the main Druze political force in Lebanon, which shows continuity from 19th century notable families to modern party politics.

Model ID

The Druze are a closed religious community that emerged from Ismaili Shi'ism in 11th century Fatimid Egypt and settled mainly in Mount Lebanon and southern Syria. In Mount Lebanon, Druze lords dominated a largely Maronite peasantry. Ottoman reform, European patronage, and Maronite growth upset that order in the 19th century, leading to violence that peaked in 1860. The settlement that followed created an autonomous Mount Lebanon governed through sect-based representation. The Druze case shows how religious identity became politically organized through changes in land, status, and outside intervention.

Maronite

Thread: religion and politics
What happened

An Eastern Catholic church centered in Mount Lebanon and in communion with Rome. That tie gave Maronites a long relationship with France, which presented itself as protector of Catholics in the Ottoman lands.

Why it mattered
  • 1860: after Druze attacks on Christians in Mount Lebanon and Damascus, France sent troops.
  • 1861: the Ottomans and the European powers created an autonomous Mount Lebanon with a Christian governor and an administrative council seated by sect, an early case of building sect into political institutions.
  • French mandate (M5): France drew the borders of Greater Lebanon to give Christians a state in which they were dominant.
  • outside context 1943 National Pact: an unwritten arrangement reserving the presidency for a Maronite, the premiership for a Sunni, and the speakership for a Shi'i.
Actors

The Maronite church, France, the Ottoman state, and Lebanese political families.

Connections

Druze and sectarianism (this module), the Eastern Question (M3), mandate (M5).

Interpretations

Maronite political power can be read as protection of a vulnerable minority, or as a privileged position built and preserved by a European patron. Both readings agree that outside powers shaped the community's political role.

Today

Lebanon's president is still, by convention, a Maronite. A 19th century arrangement survives in a 21st century state, but its persistence depends on institutions and bargains, not on history alone.

Model ID

The Maronites are an Eastern Catholic community centered in Mount Lebanon and in communion with Rome, which gave them a long tie to France. After the 1860 violence between Druze and Christians, France intervened, and the 1861 settlement made Mount Lebanon autonomous under a Christian governor with a council seated by sect. Under the mandate, France drew Greater Lebanon's borders to favor Christians, and the 1943 National Pact reserved the presidency for a Maronite. Compared with the Druze, the Maronites shared the same mountain but had a stronger European patron. Their history shows how outside powers helped write sect into the political institutions of Lebanon.

Ulama

Thread: religion and politics
What happened

The scholars of Islamic law and religious sciences. They staffed courts, taught in madrasas, administered religious endowments (waqf), and interpreted sharia.

Why it mattered

The key comparison is between states that absorbed the ulama and a state that could not.

  • Ottoman Empire and Egypt: the ulama were part of the state hierarchy under the Shaykh al-Islam. In the 19th century the state took over more of their functions through new law codes, state courts, and state schools, and they lost independent power.
  • Iran: senior Shi'i scholars, the mujtahids, had their own income from religious taxes, and the Qajar state was too weak to absorb them. The ulama could therefore lead opposition, as in the Tobacco Protest of 1891 to 1892 and the Constitutional Revolution of 1905 to 1906.
Actors

Ottoman and Egyptian state reformers, the Shaykh al-Islam, Iranian mujtahids, and Qajar shahs.

Connections

Defensive developmentalism, Tanzimat, and constitutionalism (M3), Ataturk and Reza Shah (M5).

Interpretations

The useful variable is how far the state absorbed religious functions. The more it absorbed, the less independent power the ulama had to mobilize society. A competing view stresses doctrine instead: that Shi'i theology itself made Iranian clerics more political. The structural view explains timing better, since Iranian clerics became politically decisive when the state was weak and foreign concessions angered merchants.

Today

outside context Iran's system of rule by a senior jurist sits at one extreme of ulama power. Egypt's al-Azhar, funded by the state and usually aligned with the regime, sits at the other.

Model ID

The ulama are the scholars of Islamic law who traditionally ran courts, schools, and religious endowments. In the Ottoman Empire and Egypt they were part of the state hierarchy, and 19th century reforms absorbed many of their functions into state courts and schools, weakening their independence. In Iran, Shi'i mujtahids kept independent income and faced a weak Qajar state. As a result, Iranian ulama could lead mass opposition, as in the Tobacco Protest of 1891 to 1892 and the Constitutional Revolution of 1905 to 1906. The contrast shows that the degree of state absorption, more than doctrine alone, shaped the political role of religious scholars.

Islamism vs. Islam

Thread: religion and politics
What happened

Islam is a religious tradition of belief, worship, law, and ethics. Islamism is a modern political ideology holding that Islam should be the basis of the political order, usually by using state power to apply sharia.

Why it happened

Islamism needs a modern state to capture or reform. It emerged in the 20th century as a response to colonial rule, to secular nationalist regimes, and later to their failures. The Muslim Brotherhood, founded in Egypt in 1928, is the founding organization (M4). The 1967 defeat of Arab nationalist regimes is often treated as the moment Islamism became a mass alternative (M6).

Actors

Hasan al-Banna and the Muslim Brotherhood, later Islamist parties and movements, and the secular regimes they opposed.

Connections

Salafism, Wahhabism, and the Muslim Brotherhood (M4), Nasser and pan-Arabism (M6), Hamas (M6).

Interpretations
  • Essentialist outside context, associated with writers such as Bernard Lewis: Islam does not distinguish religion from politics, so Islamism is Islam's natural politics.
  • Contextual: Islamism is a modern response to modern conditions such as state expansion, colonialism, and economic grievance, and it varies widely. Some Islamist parties compete in elections; jihadist groups reject them.
  • Your rebuttal to essentialism: a constant cannot explain variation. If Islam produced Islamism, why does Islamism appear only in the 20th century, and why does it take such different forms across countries?
Today

outside context Tunisia's Ennahda party competed in elections after 2011 and shared power, while Egypt's Brotherhood won the presidency in 2012 and was removed in 2013. Same ideological family, different strategies and outcomes.

Precision

In the 19th century the expanding state absorbed religious functions, and that produced the differentiation of religious and political spheres. Write "Middle Eastern states," not "Islamic states."

Model ID

Islam is a religious tradition, while Islamism is a modern political ideology that seeks to make Islam the basis of the state and law. Islamism emerged in the 20th century, most clearly with the Muslim Brotherhood in Egypt in 1928, as a response to colonial rule and to the expanding modern state. It gained mass support after secular Arab nationalism was discredited by the 1967 defeat. Islamism varies widely, from parties that contest elections to jihadist groups that reject them. That variation across time and place undercuts the essentialist claim that Islamism is simply Islam's natural politics.

Sectarianism

Thread: identity and nationalism
What happened

Sectarianism is the politicization of religious identity, so that sect becomes the basis of political representation, mobilization, or conflict. That is different from simply having different sects.

Why it happened

Institutions that allocate power by sect give people reasons to organize by sect. Mount Lebanon after 1861 and Lebanon's 1943 National Pact are the clearest cases.

Actors

Ottoman reformers, European powers, local notables, and later party leaders and regimes.

Connections

Druze and Maronite (this module), Tanzimat and Osmanlilik (M3), mandate (M5).

Today

outside context Lebanon's 2019 protests targeted the sectarian power-sharing system itself. Sectarian institutions can outlast public support for them, which shows that institutions persist without fully determining what people want.

Model ID

Sectarianism is the politicization of religious identity, where sect becomes the basis for representation, mobilization, or conflict. In the Middle East it is better understood as modern than ancient. After the 1860 violence in Mount Lebanon, the 1861 settlement created a council seated by sect, and the 1943 National Pact divided Lebanon's top offices among Maronites, Sunnis, and Shi'is. These institutions gave people strong reasons to organize politically by sect. Sectarianism therefore reflects how states and outside powers organized power, not a fixed hatred between communities.

Synthesis

How these terms connect, 7th century onward

Module 1 is less a single period than the background for every other module. The terms connect through one idea: religious difference becomes political only when states, institutions, or movements make it so.

The Sunni and Shi'i divide began over leadership in the 7th century, but it became a political boundary when the Safavids adopted Twelver Shi'ism in 1501. Smaller communities such as the Druze and Maronites lived side by side in Mount Lebanon for centuries; their conflict in 1860 came when Ottoman reform, European patronage, and economic change upset the old order, and the 1861 settlement turned sect into a basis for representation. That is sectarianism: the politicization of religious identity through institutions.

The ulama show the same logic from another angle. Where the state absorbed religious functions, as in the Ottoman Empire and Egypt, the ulama lost independent power. Where it could not, as in Qajar Iran, they remained able to lead opposition. Islamism, finally, is a 20th century political ideology aimed at the modern state, which is why it appears when it does and varies so much across countries.

Safavid state → Sunni/Shi'i political boundary

A state decision in 1501 turned a theological difference into an imperial frontier and the basis of Iran's identity.

Druze and Maronite → sectarianism

The 1860 conflict and the 1861 sect-based council became the model for Lebanon's confessional system.

State absorption → weakened or independent ulama

How far the state took over courts, schools, and endowments decided whether religious scholars could mobilize society.

Ulama and the modern state → Islamism

As the state absorbed religious functions, Islamist movements arose to capture or reshape that state, which makes Islamism modern.

Every term → the anti-essentialist argument

Each case shows that religion matters through politics. A constant faith cannot explain changing conflicts, institutions, and movements.

One-sentence takeaway for essays

Religious identity in the Middle East became politically decisive through state decisions and institutions, from the Safavid adoption of Shi'ism to Lebanon's sect-based councils to the modern state that Islamists sought to capture, not through religion alone.
