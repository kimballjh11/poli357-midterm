European pressure and the 19th-century response

This is the heart of the course. European power grows, Middle Eastern rulers reform their states to survive, and the reforms themselves create debt, new elites, and new ideas. Ten terms, one loop: pressure produces reform, and reform produces new openings for pressure.

Economic Capitulations

Thread: the world economy
What happened

Capitulations were commercial and legal privileges that Ottoman sultans granted to the merchants of European states: lower customs duties, exemption from certain taxes, and the right to be judged by their own consuls instead of Ottoman courts. Grants to Venice and others came early, and the French capitulations of 1569 became a model. In 1740 a new treaty renewed and expanded the French privileges, marking the height of French influence.

How the meaning changed
  • From strength: early capitulations were favors granted by a powerful sultan to attract trade and allies.
  • To weakness: as the balance of power shifted, European consuls extended protection to local, mostly non-Muslim merchants through certificates called berats. These protégés escaped Ottoman taxes and courts, which gave them an edge over Muslim merchants.
  • Commercial treaties: the Anglo-Ottoman Commercial Convention of Balta Liman (1838) was imposed on the Ottomans. It abolished state monopolies and fixed low duties, 3 percent on imports. It applied to Egypt as Ottoman territory, threatening Muhammad Ali's state monopolies; he resisted until the Ottomans and Britain reasserted control in 1840 to 1841.
End

The Ottomans abolished the capitulations unilaterally in 1914. Turkey's abolition was confirmed at Lausanne in 1923. In Egypt, the Montreux Convention of 1937 abolished them, with a transition period that ended in 1949.

Actors

Ottoman sultans, European consuls and merchants, local protégé merchants, and Britain as the main force behind Balta Liman.

Connections

Commercial Revolution and core/periphery (M2), defensive developmentalism and imperialism (this module), sectarianism (M1), Ataturk and Lausanne (M5).

Interpretations
  • One view: capitulations were a reasonable trade policy that became harmful only when Ottoman power declined.
  • Another: whatever their origins, by the 19th century they were instruments of informal empire, carving foreign enclaves out of Ottoman sovereignty.
  • Both agree that the same legal tool meant opposite things under different balances of power.
Today
outside context In 1964 Ayatollah Khomeini denounced an Iranian law granting legal immunity to American military personnel as a revival of capitulations, and he was exiled for it. The memory of capitulations still shapes sensitivity to foreign legal privilege.
Model ID

Capitulations were commercial and legal privileges, such as low customs duties and the right to be tried in consular courts, that Ottoman sultans granted to European merchants, with the French grant of 1569 serving as a model. They began as favors from a position of Ottoman strength meant to attract trade. As European power grew, consuls extended protection to local non-Muslim merchants, who escaped Ottoman taxes and courts. The 1838 Balta Liman convention, imposed on the Ottomans and applied to Egypt, abolished monopolies and fixed low tariffs. Capitulations thus became a tool of informal European control until their abolition in 1914 and at Lausanne in 1923.

The Eastern Question and the Great Game

Thread: sovereignty and the state system
What happened

The Eastern Question was the 19th century diplomatic problem of what to do about the weakening Ottoman Empire: who would gain its territory, and how to keep its decline from upsetting the European balance of power. Britain usually propped up the Ottomans to block Russia and protect the route to India. Russia sought access to the Straits and posed as protector of Orthodox Christians. France claimed to protect Catholics. Austria watched the Balkans.

  • Greek independence (recognized 1830)
  • Crimean War (1853 to 1856) and the Treaty of Paris (1856)
  • Russo-Turkish War (1877 to 1878), the Treaty of San Stefano, and the Congress of Berlin (1878), where Britain took Cyprus

The Great Game was the Anglo-Russian rivalry over Central Asia, Afghanistan, and Iran, from the early 19th century until the Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907. That convention divided Iran into a Russian sphere in the north, a British sphere in the southeast, and a neutral zone between them.

Why it happened

Britain feared Russian expansion toward India and the Mediterranean. Russia sought warm-water access and influence. Both preferred weak buffer states to direct conflict with each other.

Actors

Britain, Russia, France, Austria, the Ottoman government, and the Qajar shahs.

Connections

Westphalia (M2), capitulations and protectorate (this module), Druze and Maronite (M1), World War I agreements (M5).

Interpretations
  • Realist: the region was an object of great-power balancing, and its states survived because the powers checked each other.
  • Local agency: Ottoman and Qajar rulers played the powers against each other to keep some independence, which is why the Ottoman Empire and Iran were never fully colonized.
Today
outside context Russia's military intervention in Syria from 2015 and its naval base at Tartus echoed the old search for a warm-water foothold. After Assad's fall in December 2024, the future of those bases became uncertain, which shows how such footholds depend on local politics.
Model ID

The Eastern Question was the 19th century problem among European powers of how to manage the decline of the Ottoman Empire, while the Great Game was the Anglo-Russian rivalry over Iran, Afghanistan, and Central Asia. In the Eastern Question, Britain generally propped up the Ottomans against Russia, as in the Crimean War of 1853 to 1856. In the Great Game, the 1907 Anglo-Russian Convention divided Iran into Russian and British spheres. Both treated the region as an object of European strategy rather than as sovereign actors. Yet great-power rivalry also helped the Ottoman Empire and Iran survive as buffers rather than becoming colonies.

Defensive Developmentalism

Thread: the modern state
What it means

Gelvin's term for the policies Middle Eastern rulers adopted to strengthen their states against European encroachment and internal rivals: modern conscript armies, centralized administration, tax reform, state factories, schools, and sending students to Europe.

Cases
  • Ottoman Empire: Selim III's New Order army in the 1790s provoked a Janissary revolt and his overthrow in 1807. Mahmud II destroyed the Janissaries in 1826 and centralized power, preparing the way for the Tanzimat.
  • Egypt: Muhammad Ali, an Ottoman officer of Albanian origin, rose in the vacuum left by the French withdrawal in 1801 and ruled from 1805 to 1848. He conscripted peasants, created state monopolies and factories, sent students to France, and invaded Syria in 1831.
  • Iran: chief minister Amir Kabir (1848 to 1851) attempted army, tax, and education reform and founded the Dar al-Funun school in 1851, but he was dismissed and killed.
Why it happened

Military defeats showed that the old order could not resist European armies, and rulers also needed strength against internal rivals. Muhammad Ali's army was aimed at the Ottoman sultan as much as at Europe.

Results
  • Success: it built stronger, more centralized states and created new educated elites: officers, bureaucrats, and teachers.
  • Failure: reforms were expensive. Borrowing led to debt and then foreign control. Tunisia accepted an international debt commission in 1869 and became a French protectorate in 1881. Egypt went bankrupt in 1876, and Britain occupied it in 1882.
  • Unintended effect: the new elites trained by the state later demanded constitutions to limit rulers.
Actors

Selim III, Mahmud II, Muhammad Ali, Ottoman Tanzimat statesmen, Amir Kabir, and European creditors.

Connections

Ottoman Empire and core/periphery (M2), ulama (M1), Tanzimat, constitutionalism, imperialism, and protectorate (this module), Ataturk and Reza Shah as later versions (M5).

Interpretations
  • A useful formulation: defensive developmentalism succeeded at building modern states and failed at preserving their independence. Reform and imperialism ran in a loop: reform required money, money brought debt, and debt brought control.
  • Muhammad Ali as nationalist modernizer or dynast: outside context Egyptian nationalist history cast him as the founder of modern Egypt. Khaled Fahmy and others argue he was an Ottoman dynast building power for his family, using Egyptian peasants as conscripts.
Today
outside context Gulf monarchies' state-led development plans, such as Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030, are top-down programs meant to secure regimes by modernizing economies. The logic resembles defensive developmentalism, but oil money changes the financing problem that sank 19th century reformers.
Model ID

Defensive developmentalism is Gelvin's term for the reforms rulers adopted to strengthen their states against Europe and internal rivals, including conscript armies, centralized bureaucracy, tax reform, and state schools. Examples include Mahmud II's destruction of the Janissaries in 1826 and Muhammad Ali's conscript army and state monopolies in Egypt after 1805. These programs built stronger states and created new educated elites. But they were expensive, and borrowing led to debt and foreign control, as in Egypt's bankruptcy in 1876 and British occupation in 1882. Defensive developmentalism therefore succeeded at building modern states while often failing to preserve their independence.

Tanzimat Reforms

Thread: the modern state
What happened

The Tanzimat ("reorganization") was the period of Ottoman reform from 1839 to 1876.

  • Gulhane Edict (1839): issued under Sultan Abdulmecid and associated with Mustafa Resid Pasha. It promised security of life, honor, and property, regular taxation instead of tax farming, and orderly conscription.
  • Reform Edict (1856): issued at the end of the Crimean War under European pressure, it promised explicit equality between Muslim and non-Muslim subjects.
  • Institutions: new commercial and penal codes, the Land Code of 1858, the provincial law of 1864, state (nizamiye) courts, state schools, and the Mecelle civil code (1869 to 1876), which codified Islamic law in a modern format.
  • The period ended with the constitution of 1876.
Why it happened

To centralize revenue and power, to build a loyal citizenry, and to remove the pretext for European intervention on behalf of Christian subjects.

Effects
  • A larger, more centralized bureaucracy with more reach into the provinces.
  • Equality for non-Muslims angered some Muslims who lost their privileged status and some Christian clergy who lost authority over their communities.
  • The reforms disrupted local hierarchies and are linked to the violence in Mount Lebanon and Damascus in 1860.
  • The state absorbed legal and educational functions from the ulama.
Actors

Sultans Abdulmecid and Abdulaziz, Mustafa Resid Pasha, Ali Pasha and Fuad Pasha, European ambassadors, and provincial elites.

Connections

Ulama, Druze and Maronite, sectarianism (M1), defensive developmentalism, Land Code, Osmanlilik, constitutionalism (this module), Kemalism (M5).

Interpretations
  • Westernization: reforms copied from Europe under European pressure.
  • Ottoman continuity: reforms built on earlier Ottoman traditions of centralization and justice, and Ottoman statesmen chose them for their own purposes.
  • Liberal or absolutist: the edicts used the language of rights, but their main effect was to strengthen the central state.
Today

Turkey's tradition of top-down, state-led reform runs from the Tanzimat through Kemalism. Continuity does not mean destiny: the same centralized state has served very different governments.

Model ID

The Tanzimat was the period of Ottoman reform from 1839 to 1876, opened by the Gulhane Edict of 1839 and extended by the Reform Edict of 1856, which promised equality for Muslim and non-Muslim subjects. It created new law codes, state courts and schools, the Land Code of 1858, and the Mecelle civil code. The reforms aimed to centralize revenue and power and to remove European pretexts for intervening on behalf of Christians. They expanded the bureaucracy and absorbed functions once held by the ulama, but equality decrees disrupted local hierarchies and are linked to the 1860 violence in Mount Lebanon and Damascus. The Tanzimat is the Ottoman form of defensive developmentalism and led directly to the constitution of 1876.

Ottoman Land Code of 1858

Thread: the world economy
What happened

Part of the Tanzimat, the code classified land into categories, most importantly state-owned land (miri) held by cultivators, and required holders to register their land and receive title deeds.

Why it happened

To increase tax revenue, to identify who was responsible for taxes, to tie cultivators directly to the state, and to cut out intermediaries.

What actually happened
  • Results varied by region.
  • In many areas, especially in Syria, Iraq, and Palestine, peasants avoided registration because they feared taxes and conscription, or communal land was registered in the names of tribal sheikhs, urban notables, and merchants.
  • The result was often large estates and absentee landlords, with former owners becoming tenants.
  • outside context In Palestine, some absentee owners later sold land to Zionist organizations; the Sursock family of Beirut sold land in the Jezreel Valley in the 1920s, and tenant farmers were displaced.
Actors

Ottoman reformers, peasants, tribal sheikhs, urban notables and merchants, and later land buyers.

Connections

Core/periphery (M2), Tanzimat (this module), Zionism and aliyah (M4), Nasser's and the Ba'ath's land reforms (M6).

Interpretations
  • Classic view: the code concentrated land in the hands of notables and created a landed elite.
  • Revisionist view: outside context historians such as Martha Mundy show the outcomes varied widely, and in some places smallholders did register and benefit.
  • Either way, the code turned land into a registered commodity linked to the export economy.
Today
outside context Ottoman land categories and records still matter in land disputes in Israel and the West Bank. A 19th century tax reform remains part of a 21st century conflict.
Model ID

The Ottoman Land Code of 1858 was a Tanzimat reform that classified land and required cultivators to register their holdings and receive title deeds. Its goals were to raise tax revenue and tie cultivators directly to the state. In many areas, especially Syria, Iraq, and Palestine, peasants avoided registration out of fear of taxes and conscription, and land was registered to sheikhs, notables, and merchants. This often produced large estates and absentee landlords, though revisionist historians stress regional variation. The code shaped the landed elites later targeted by land reform and the land sales that affected Palestinian tenant farmers.

Osmanlilik

Thread: identity and nationalism
What it means

Ottomanism: the idea of a common Ottoman identity and citizenship for all subjects regardless of religion or ethnicity, meant to bind them to the state.

Why it happened

To counter separatist nationalism among Balkan Christians and to remove the pretext for European intervention. It grew from the equality promised in the edicts of 1839 and 1856, was formalized in the Ottoman Nationality Law of 1869, and was championed by the Young Ottomans, such as Namik Kemal, who linked it to constitutional government.

Why it faded
  • Balkan nationalisms continued, and many Christians preferred European protection or independence.
  • Some Muslims resented losing their privileged status.
  • After losing much of the Christian Balkans in 1878, Abdulhamid II leaned more on Islamic solidarity and his role as caliph.
  • After 1908, and especially after the Balkan Wars of 1912 to 1913, the Young Turks moved toward Turkish nationalism.
Actors

Tanzimat statesmen, the Young Ottomans, Abdulhamid II, the Young Turks, and Balkan nationalists.

Connections

Sectarianism (M1), Tanzimat and constitutionalism (this module), nationalism (M4), Kemalism (M5).

Interpretations
  • Genuine civic project: an early attempt at equal citizenship that could have held a diverse empire together.
  • Tool of centralization: a way to tie subjects directly to the state and weaken community leaders.
  • Its failure is often used to argue that imperial civic identity could not compete with ethnic nationalism, though the outcome also depended on wars and European intervention.
Today
outside context Turkey's long conflict over Kurdish identity is a version of the same question: can a single civic identity include groups with distinct languages and histories?
Model ID

Osmanlilik, or Ottomanism, was the idea of a shared Ottoman identity and citizenship for all subjects regardless of religion or ethnicity. It grew from the equality promised in the Tanzimat edicts of 1839 and 1856, was formalized in the Nationality Law of 1869, and was championed by the Young Ottomans alongside constitutionalism. Its goal was to bind diverse subjects to the state and undercut separatism and European intervention. It faded as Balkan nationalisms advanced, as Abdulhamid II emphasized Islamic solidarity after 1878, and as the Young Turks turned toward Turkish nationalism after the Balkan Wars. Its failure helped clear the way for the nation-states that followed the empire.

Constitutionalism

Thread: the modern state
What it means

Movements to limit a ruler's power through a written constitution and a representative assembly.

Cases
  • Tunisia (1861): often cited as the first constitution in the Muslim world; suspended in 1864.
  • Ottoman Empire (1876): drafted under Midhat Pasha, suspended by Abdulhamid II in 1878, and restored by the Young Turk Revolution of 1908, led by the Committee of Union and Progress.
  • Egypt: a constitutional movement and the Urabi revolt (1881 to 1882) challenged the khedive and foreign financial control and ended with the British occupation of 1882.
  • Iran (1905 to 1911): a coalition of bazaar merchants, ulama, and intellectuals won a parliament (majlis) and constitution in 1906. Russian intervention closed the parliament in 1911.
Why it happened
  • State schools and armies created new elites who knew European political ideas.
  • Fiscal crises and foreign control made limiting the ruler's spending and concessions urgent.
  • Constitutions seemed to be a source of European strength.
Actors

Midhat Pasha, the Young Ottomans and Young Turks, Ahmed Urabi, Iranian merchants, ulama, and intellectuals, and the European powers that intervened.

Connections

Ulama (M1), defensive developmentalism, Tanzimat, Osmanlilik (this module), Reza Shah (M5).

Interpretations
  • A strong explanation: three factors drove constitutionalism: state-built institutions that produced new educated elites, state absorption of religious functions, and foreign control over state revenue. The degree of absorption varied, which explains why Ottoman and Egyptian constitutionalism stayed elite while Iran's mobilized a mass coalition that included independent ulama.
  • Western import: constitutionalism as borrowed European liberalism.
  • Indigenous roots: reformers justified it with Islamic ideas such as consultation (shura) and defended it as a way to protect the community.
Today
outside context Constitutions in the region have often been rewritten to strengthen executives. Tunisia's 2022 constitution, adopted under President Kais Saied, concentrated power in the presidency. A constitution's text does not by itself limit a ruler.
Model ID

Constitutionalism was the movement to limit rulers through written constitutions and representative assemblies, seen in Tunisia in 1861, the Ottoman constitution of 1876, and Iran's Constitutional Revolution of 1905 to 1906. It was driven by new elites trained in state schools and armies, by fiscal crises and foreign control of revenue, and by the belief that constitutions explained European strength. Ottoman constitutionalism was suspended in 1878 and restored by the Young Turks in 1908. Iran's movement drew a mass coalition of merchants, ulama, and intellectuals because the Iranian ulama were independent of the state. The difference shows that the degree of state absorption of religious institutions shaped how broad constitutional movements became.

Colonialism

Thread: sovereignty and the state system
What it means

Direct rule over a territory by a foreign power, often with settlement by people from the ruling country.

Cases
  • French Algeria (1830 to 1962): conquered from 1830, later treated as part of France, with a large European settler population and extensive land confiscation. Independence came in 1962 after a war that began in 1954.
  • Italian Libya: invaded in 1911, with brutal suppression of resistance in the 1920s and early 1930s.
  • British Aden: occupied in 1839 as a coaling station on the route to India.
Why it happened

Strategic routes, prestige, markets, and land for settlers. Algeria began partly as a French political adventure and became a settler colony.

Actors

European states and armies, settlers, and local resistance movements.

Connections

Imperialism and protectorate (this module), Zionism (M4), mandate (M5).

Interpretations
  • Colonialism is usually distinguished from the broader category of imperialism by direct rule and settlement.
  • outside context Some scholars analyze Zionism as settler colonialism, while others reject the label because Jewish immigrants saw themselves as returning to an ancestral homeland and lacked a sponsoring mother country. The label is contested; present it as a debate.
Today
outside context France and Algeria still argue over memory of the colonial period and the independence war, and the issue shapes their diplomatic relations.
Model ID

Colonialism is the direct rule of a territory by a foreign power, often with settlement by colonists. The clearest Middle Eastern case is French Algeria, conquered from 1830, treated as part of France, and settled by Europeans until independence in 1962 after an eight-year war. Other cases include Italian Libya from 1911 and British Aden from 1839. Colonialism differs from imperialism, which also includes indirect and economic control. Settler colonialism in particular produced land loss, legal hierarchies between settlers and natives, and violent decolonization.

Imperialism

Thread: sovereignty and the state system
What it means

The broader extension of power over other peoples. It includes direct colonial rule but also informal empire: control through trade treaties, debt, concessions, spheres of influence, and protectorates.

Cases
  • Trade: the Balta Liman convention of 1838.
  • Debt: Egypt's international debt commission of 1876 and the British occupation of 1882; the Ottoman Public Debt Administration of 1881, which collected certain state revenues for European bondholders.
  • Concessions: in Iran, the Reuter concession (1872), the tobacco concession (1890), and the D'Arcy oil concession (1901), which led to the Anglo-Persian Oil Company in 1909.
  • Spheres: the Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907.
Why it happened (theories)
  • Economic: outside context J. A. Hobson and Lenin argued that the search for markets and investment outlets drove empire.
  • Strategic: outside context Robinson and Gallagher stressed the protection of routes to India and informal empire through free trade.
  • Local crisis: crises on the periphery, such as Egypt's bankruptcy and the Urabi revolt, pulled reluctant powers into direct control.
Actors

European governments, banks, bondholders, concessionaires, and the local rulers who borrowed and granted concessions.

Connections

Core/periphery (M2), capitulations, defensive developmentalism, protectorate, colonialism (this module), mandate (M5), oil (M6).

Interpretations

The key course interpretation is that imperialism and defensive developmentalism ran in a loop. Reform required money, money required loans, and default justified foreign control.

Today
outside context Many governments in the region still frame their legitimacy as resistance to imperialism, as in the Islamic Republic of Iran's hostility to the United States. The rhetoric is a resource leaders use, not proof that the past dictates present policy.
Model ID

Imperialism is the extension of power over other peoples, including direct colonial rule and informal control through trade treaties, debt, concessions, and spheres of influence. In the Middle East it took the form of the 1838 Balta Liman convention, the Ottoman Public Debt Administration of 1881, and concessions in Iran such as the 1901 D'Arcy oil concession. Egypt shows the mechanism clearly: bankruptcy in 1876 brought European financial control and then British occupation in 1882. Scholars explain imperialism through economic interests, strategic routes to India, or crises on the periphery. In the course frame, imperialism and defensive developmentalism fed each other in a loop of reform, debt, and control.

Protectorate

Thread: sovereignty and the state system
What it means

An arrangement in which a local ruler keeps nominal sovereignty while a foreign power controls foreign affairs and defense, and often finances and administration.

Cases
  • Tunisia: French protectorate from the Treaty of Bardo in 1881.
  • Morocco: French protectorate from 1912, with a Spanish zone in the north.
  • Egypt: under British occupation from 1882 while formally still Ottoman, sometimes called a veiled protectorate. Britain declared a formal protectorate in 1914 when the Ottomans entered World War I and granted limited independence in 1922.
  • Gulf sheikhdoms: under treaties from 1820 onward, and exclusive agreements from 1892, Britain controlled the foreign relations of the Trucial States, Bahrain, and later Kuwait and Qatar, until withdrawals that ended in 1971.
Why it happened

Protectorates gave control at lower cost than direct rule and kept local rulers as intermediaries.

Actors

Britain, France, Spain, and local beys, sultans, khedives, and sheikhs.

Connections

Imperialism and colonialism (this module), mandate (M5), rentier state (M6).

Compared with
  • Colony: direct annexation and rule, as in Algeria.
  • Mandate (M5): supervised by the League of Nations, with the stated goal of preparing for independence.
Today

The ruling families of Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, and the UAE were preserved under British protection and still rule today. Protection helped freeze local dynasties in place, though oil wealth later explains much of their durability.

Model ID

A protectorate is an arrangement in which a local ruler keeps nominal sovereignty while a foreign power controls foreign affairs, defense, and often finances. France established protectorates over Tunisia in 1881 and Morocco in 1912, and Britain declared a formal protectorate over Egypt in 1914 after ruling it as a veiled protectorate since 1882. Britain also controlled the foreign relations of the Gulf sheikhdoms through treaties until 1971. Protectorates differed from colonies like Algeria, which were annexed outright, and from mandates, which were supervised by the League of Nations. The Gulf protectorates preserved the ruling families that still govern those states today.

Synthesis

How these terms connect, c. 1800 to 1914

In the 19th century, European power, built in the early modern period, arrived in force. It came through diplomacy (the Eastern Question and the Great Game), through commerce (capitulations and the Balta Liman convention), and eventually through direct control (colonialism in Algeria, protectorates in Tunisia and Egypt).

Middle Eastern rulers responded with defensive developmentalism. In the Ottoman Empire that meant the Tanzimat, including the Land Code of 1858, and a new civic identity, Osmanlilik, meant to hold a diverse empire together. In Egypt it meant Muhammad Ali's army and monopolies. In Iran, where the Qajar state was weak, reform barely got started.

The reforms had three effects that drive the rest of the course. They built stronger states. They created debt, which brought European financial control and, in Egypt and Tunisia, occupation. And they created new educated elites who demanded constitutions to limit rulers and stop foreign concessions. Where the state had absorbed the ulama, constitutionalism stayed elite; where it had not, as in Iran, it became a mass movement.

Eastern Question → survival and intervention

Great-power rivalry kept the Ottoman Empire and Iran alive as buffers but gave Europeans constant reasons to intervene, as France did in Mount Lebanon in 1860.

Capitulations and Balta Liman → weak fiscal base

Low tariffs and foreign legal privileges limited Ottoman and Egyptian revenue and undercut state monopolies.

Defensive developmentalism → debt → imperialism

Reform required loans; default brought the Ottoman Public Debt Administration, Egypt's occupation in 1882, and Tunisia's protectorate in 1881.

Tanzimat → Land Code and Osmanlilik

The same reform program reorganized land to raise revenue and promised equal citizenship to bind subjects to the state.

Tanzimat equality → sectarian tension

Equality decrees and European patronage disrupted local hierarchies, contributing to the 1860 violence in Mount Lebanon and Damascus.

Reform → new elites → constitutionalism

State schools and armies produced the officers and bureaucrats who led the 1876 constitution and the 1908 revolution.

Imperialism → colonialism and protectorates

Informal control could turn into formal control when local crises gave Europeans a reason, as in Egypt and Tunisia.

One-sentence takeaway for essays

In the 19th century, defensive developmentalism and imperialism ran in a loop: reform built modern states, financing reform created debt, and debt invited foreign control.
