Module 6 · 1940s to 2000s

The postwar state, oil, and the U.S.

After independence, officers and parties seized power promising unity, socialism, and liberation. Oil gave some states enormous revenue, and the international economic order built at Bretton Woods shaped how that money moved. Palestinian movements shifted from secular nationalism to Islamism, and U.S. doctrine shifted from economic reform to military democracy promotion. Nine terms, four threads.

Nasser

Thread: the modern state
What happened
  • Gamal Abdel Nasser (1918 to 1970) led the Free Officers who overthrew King Farouk on July 23, 1952. He became prime minister in 1954 and president in 1956.
  • Land reform in 1952 limited holdings and broke the old landed elite. Political parties were banned in 1953, and the Muslim Brotherhood was crushed in 1954.
  • He joined the non-aligned movement at Bandung (1955) and bought Soviet-bloc arms through Czechoslovakia (1955).
  • When the U.S. and Britain withdrew funding for the Aswan High Dam, he nationalized the Suez Canal Company in July 1956. Israel, Britain, and France invaded, but U.S. and Soviet pressure forced them out. The Suez crisis made Nasser a hero across the Arab world.
  • He united Egypt with Syria as the United Arab Republic (1958 to 1961), nationalized large parts of the economy in 1961 under Arab socialism, and intervened in Yemen's civil war (1962 to 1967).
  • The June 1967 war with Israel was a catastrophic defeat; Egypt lost Sinai and Gaza. He died in September 1970.
Why it happened

The old regime was discredited by British influence, the 1948 defeat in Palestine, corruption, and land inequality. The army was the best organized national institution.

How the regime worked

The state provided jobs, subsidies, land, and education, including guaranteed public jobs for graduates, while banning independent political activity. outside context Scholars call this an authoritarian bargain.

Actors

Nasser and the Free Officers, the army, the Muslim Brotherhood, Britain, France, Israel, the U.S., and the USSR.

Connections

Muslim Brotherhood (M4), Kemalism (M5), pan-Arabism, Ba'athism, PLO (this module).

Interpretations
  • Anti-imperial liberator and developer who expanded education, land ownership, and national dignity.
  • Authoritarian who built a security state, wasted resources on regional adventures, and led Egypt to defeat in 1967.
  • 1967 is the turning point most analysts use to explain the decline of secular Arab nationalism and the rise of Islamism.
Today
outside context Apart from Mohamed Morsi and two brief interim leaders, every Egyptian president since 1952 has come from the military, including Abdel Fattah el-Sisi. The military-dominated state Nasser built has outlasted his ideology.
Model ID

Gamal Abdel Nasser led the Free Officers coup of 1952 that overthrew Egypt's monarchy and ruled as president from 1956 until his death in 1970. He enacted land reform, banned parties, and crushed the Muslim Brotherhood. His nationalization of the Suez Canal Company in 1956 and political victory in the Suez crisis made him the leader of pan-Arabism, and he united Egypt and Syria in the United Arab Republic from 1958 to 1961. His regime offered jobs, subsidies, and education in exchange for political quiet. The 1967 defeat discredited his model and opened space for Islamism, but the military-dominated state he built still rules Egypt.

Pan-Arabism

Thread: identity and nationalism
What it means

The idea that Arabs form one nation, defined by shared language, history, and culture, and should unite politically or at least act together, overcoming borders drawn by imperial powers.

What happened
  • Roots: the 19th century Arabic cultural revival, the Arab Revolt as symbol, and interwar thinkers such as Sati' al-Husri, who stressed language and history.
  • Peak: the 1950s and 1960s under Nasser, spread by Cairo's Voice of the Arabs radio. The United Arab Republic (1958 to 1961) was the high point.
  • Institutions: the Arab League (1945) was built on separate sovereign states, not unification.
  • Decline: Syria left the UAR in 1961; the 1967 defeat; Egypt's separate peace with Israel (Camp David 1978, treaty 1979); and Iraq's invasion of Kuwait (1990), an Arab state conquering another.
Why it rose and fell

It offered a powerful answer to imperial borders and the loss of Palestine. It fell because rulers would not give up their own states and power, and because defeat and rivalry exposed the gap between rhetoric and action.

Actors

Nasser, the Ba'ath Party, Arab intellectuals, the Arab League, and rival Arab monarchies.

Connections

Nationalism (M4), Arab Revolt and mandate (M5), Nasser, Ba'athism, PLO (this module).

Interpretations
  • Genuine popular identity that mobilized millions.
  • Tool of regime competition: outside context Malcolm Kerr called 1958 to 1970 the Arab Cold War, in which regimes competed for leadership using pan-Arab rhetoric.
  • outside context Fouad Ajami's essay The End of Pan-Arabism (1978) argued that state interests had defeated the ideology.
Today
outside context Arab solidarity with Palestinians remains strong in public opinion, yet the UAE, Bahrain, Morocco, and Sudan normalized relations with Israel through the Abraham Accords in 2020 and 2021. States act on their own interests even when publics share a broader identity.
Model ID

Pan-Arabism is the belief that Arabs form a single nation united by language, history, and culture and should unite politically or act together across borders drawn by imperial powers. It drew on the 19th century cultural revival and peaked under Nasser in the 1950s and 1960s, most clearly in the United Arab Republic of Egypt and Syria from 1958 to 1961. It declined after Syria's secession in 1961, the 1967 defeat, Egypt's separate peace with Israel in 1979, and Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in 1990. Pan-Arabism was a broad movement, while Ba'athism was a specific party ideology within it. Its decline shows that state sovereignty outlasted the transnational identity that challenged it.

Ba'athism

Thread: identity and nationalism
What it means

The ideology of the Arab Socialist Ba'ath ("renaissance") Party, founded in Damascus by Michel Aflaq, an Orthodox Christian, and Salah al-Din al-Bitar, a Sunni Muslim. The party held its founding congress in 1947. Its slogan was unity, freedom, socialism: one Arab nation, secular and socialist.

What happened
  • Merged with Akram al-Hawrani's Arab Socialist Party in the early 1950s.
  • Pushed Syria into the United Arab Republic in 1958, then broke with Nasser.
  • Took power by coup in Iraq (February 1963, ousted that November) and Syria (March 1963).
  • In 1966 a military faction seized control in Syria and the founders were driven out; Aflaq later went to Iraq. The Syrian and Iraqi Ba'ath parties became bitter rivals.
  • Hafez al-Assad took power in Syria in 1970. In Iraq, the Ba'ath returned in 1968, and Saddam Hussein became president in 1979.
In practice

Ba'athism became the official ideology of military and security regimes built on family, clan, and sectarian networks: Alawite officers in Syria, Sunni Arab networks from Tikrit in Iraq.

End

In Iraq, the U.S.-led occupation banned the party in 2003 through de-Ba'athification. In Syria, Bashar al-Assad's regime fell in December 2024.

Actors

Michel Aflaq, Salah al-Din al-Bitar, Hafez and Bashar al-Assad, Saddam Hussein, and party and army officers.

Connections

Sectarianism (M1), nationalism (M4), mandate (M5), Nasser, pan-Arabism, neoconservatism (this module).

Interpretations
  • Ideology mattered: Ba'athism shaped secular education, land reform, and state-led economies.
  • Ideology as veneer: outside context historians such as Hanna Batatu showed how kinship, region, and sect structured power behind the party label.
Today
outside context Many analysts argue that de-Ba'athification in Iraq in 2003 alienated Sunni Arabs and helped fuel the insurgency and later the Islamic State. The way the Ba'ath was removed shaped what came after.
Model ID

Ba'athism is the ideology of the Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party, founded in Damascus by Michel Aflaq and Salah al-Din al-Bitar and formally established in 1947, with the slogan unity, freedom, socialism. It came to power through military coups in Syria and Iraq in 1963, later consolidated under Hafez al-Assad in Syria in 1970 and the Iraqi Ba'ath from 1968, with Saddam Hussein as president from 1979. Unlike the broader pan-Arab movement led by Nasser, it was a party with its own ideology and organization, and its Syrian and Iraqi branches became rivals. In practice it became a cover for military regimes built on family, clan, and sect. Its end came with de-Ba'athification in Iraq in 2003 and the fall of the Assad regime in Syria in 2024.

PLO

Thread: identity and nationalism
What happened
  • The Palestine Liberation Organization was founded in 1964 under Arab League sponsorship, largely controlled by Egypt, with Ahmad Shuqayri as chairman.
  • After the 1967 defeat, guerrilla groups took it over. Fatah, founded in the late 1950s by Yasser Arafat and others, was the largest, and Arafat became chairman in 1969. Other factions included the Marxist Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine (PFLP).
  • The PLO was expelled from Jordan after fighting in 1970 to 1971 (Black September) and moved to Lebanon, where it became entangled in the civil war. Israel's 1982 invasion of Lebanon forced it to Tunis.
  • The 1974 Arab League summit in Rabat recognized it as the sole legitimate representative of the Palestinian people, and Arafat addressed the UN that year.
  • In 1988 the PLO declared Palestinian independence and moved toward accepting a two-state solution. In the Oslo Accords of 1993, Israel and the PLO recognized each other, and the Palestinian Authority was created in 1994.
Why it happened

Arab states created it to manage the Palestinian cause; the 1967 defeat discredited those states and let Palestinians take control of their own movement.

Actors

Yasser Arafat, Fatah, the PFLP, Arab states, Israel, and the U.S.

Connections

Zionism and aliyah (M4), Balfour and mandate (M5), Nasser, pan-Arabism, Hamas (this module).

Interpretations
  • National liberation movement that won international recognition for Palestinian nationhood.
  • Terrorist organization: the view of Israel and the U.S. for decades, based on attacks on civilians in the 1970s.
  • Oslo: a historic compromise, or a trap that created a dependent Palestinian Authority without a state.
Today
outside context The Fatah-dominated Palestinian Authority governs parts of the West Bank, but no presidential election has been held since 2005 and its legitimacy has weakened.
Model ID

The Palestine Liberation Organization was founded in 1964 under Arab League and Egyptian sponsorship, then taken over after the 1967 defeat by guerrilla factions led by Yasser Arafat's Fatah, with Arafat as chairman from 1969. It was a secular nationalist umbrella that used armed struggle and was expelled from Jordan in 1970 to 1971 and from Lebanon in 1982. The Arab League recognized it as the sole legitimate representative of Palestinians in 1974. In the 1993 Oslo Accords it recognized Israel, and the Palestinian Authority was created in 1994. Compared with Hamas, it is secular and accepted a negotiated two-state framework.

Hamas

Thread: religion and politics
What happened
  • The Islamic Resistance Movement was founded in December 1987, at the start of the First Intifada, by Muslim Brotherhood leaders in Gaza, including Sheikh Ahmed Yassin.
  • Its 1988 charter framed the conflict in religious terms and rejected Israel; it also contained antisemitic language. A 2017 document of principles accepted a state on the 1967 lines as a national consensus formula without recognizing Israel.
  • It combined social services with an armed wing, the Qassam Brigades, which carried out suicide bombings in the 1990s and 2000s in opposition to Oslo.
  • It won the 2006 Palestinian legislative elections and seized control of Gaza from Fatah in 2007. Israel and Egypt imposed a blockade.
  • outside context Wars followed in 2008 to 2009, 2012, 2014, and 2021. Hamas's October 7, 2023 attack killed about 1,200 people in Israel and took about 250 hostages, and Israel's war in Gaza that followed killed tens of thousands of Palestinians. Check current news for the latest status.
Why it happened

The Brotherhood's social networks in Gaza, frustration with the PLO's lack of results, and the Intifada's mass mobilization. outside context Israel was widely reported to have tolerated Islamist groups in the 1980s as a counterweight to the PLO.

Actors

Ahmed Yassin, Hamas political and military leaders, Fatah, Israel, Egypt, Iran, and Qatar.

Connections

Islamism (M1), Muslim Brotherhood (M4), PLO (this module).

Interpretations
  • Terrorist organization: the designation of the U.S., the EU, and others.
  • National resistance movement with an Islamist ideology: the view of its supporters.
  • Nationalist or religious: analysts debate whether Hamas is mainly a Palestinian nationalist movement in religious language or a religious movement that uses nationalism; the 2017 document is read as pragmatic evolution by some and as a tactic by others.
Today

The Fatah and Hamas split divides Palestinian governance between the West Bank and Gaza and is one of the biggest obstacles to any political settlement.

Model ID

Hamas, the Islamic Resistance Movement, was founded in Gaza in December 1987 by Muslim Brotherhood leaders, including Sheikh Ahmed Yassin, at the start of the First Intifada. It combines Islamist ideology, social services, and an armed wing, and it opposed the Oslo Accords through violence, including suicide bombings. It won the 2006 Palestinian legislative elections and took control of Gaza in 2007. Unlike the secular PLO, which recognized Israel in 1993, Hamas has not recognized Israel, though its 2017 document accepted a state on the 1967 lines as a consensus formula. Its rise reflects the shift in Palestinian politics from secular nationalism to Islamism and has divided Palestinian governance.

Bretton Woods System

Thread: the world economy
What happened
  • At a 1944 conference in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, the Allies created the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank and set up a system of fixed but adjustable exchange rates pegged to the U.S. dollar, which was convertible to gold at $35 an ounce.
  • Oil was priced in dollars.
  • On August 15, 1971, President Nixon suspended the dollar's convertibility to gold. By 1973 major currencies floated.
Effects on the Middle East
  • Oil revenue: a falling dollar cut the real value of oil revenue. Producers pushed for higher prices and, in agreements in Geneva in 1972 and 1973, won compensation for the dollar's devaluation. This is part of the background to the 1973 price shock (see OPEC).
  • Petrodollar recycling: after 1973, Gulf producers deposited huge surpluses in Western banks, which lent them to developing countries, contributing to the 1980s debt crisis.
  • IMF and World Bank: the Bretton Woods institutions later imposed structural adjustment programs on indebted states such as Egypt, Jordan, Morocco, and Tunisia (see neoliberalism).
Actors

The U.S. and Britain as architects, the IMF and World Bank, oil producers, and Western banks.

Connections

Commercial Revolution and core/periphery (M2), OPEC, rentier state, neoliberalism (this module).

Interpretations
  • Liberal order: a stable framework that enabled postwar growth.
  • Structure of dependence: a U.S.-centered system that kept producers' revenues tied to the dollar and gave Western institutions leverage over indebted states.
Today
outside context Egypt has repeatedly turned to the IMF, including a program expanded to $8 billion in 2024 alongside a large investment from the UAE. Bretton Woods institutions still shape economic policy in the region.
Model ID

The Bretton Woods system, created at a 1944 conference, set up the IMF and World Bank and fixed exchange rates to a U.S. dollar convertible to gold at $35 an ounce. Because oil was priced in dollars, Nixon's suspension of gold convertibility in 1971 and the dollar's fall cut the real value of oil revenue. Producers responded by demanding higher prices and compensation, part of the background to the 1973 oil shock. After 1973, Gulf surpluses were recycled through Western banks, and the Bretton Woods institutions later imposed structural adjustment on indebted states like Egypt and Jordan. Bretton Woods thus shaped both the oil states' revenues and the neoliberal reforms imposed on non-oil states.

Rentier State

Thread: the world economy
What it means
outside context A concept developed by Hossein Mahdavy (1970), writing on Iran, and by Hazem Beblawi and Giacomo Luciani (1987). A rentier state draws a large share of its revenue from external rents, mainly oil and gas, but also aid, canal fees, or remittances, rather than from taxing its own population.
Mechanisms
  • Taxation effect: with low or no taxes, citizens have less leverage to demand representation, the reverse of "no taxation without representation."
  • Spending effect: the state buys loyalty with public jobs, subsidies, and services.
  • Repression effect: rents fund large security forces.
  • Weak society: few independent business classes or interest groups form when the state is the main employer.
  • outside context Michael Ross's 2001 article Does Oil Hinder Democracy? tested these mechanisms statistically and found oil associated with authoritarianism.
Cases

Full rentiers: Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, the UAE, Qatar, Libya, and Iran under the shah. Semi-rentiers: Egypt (Suez Canal fees, aid, remittances) and Jordan (aid and remittances).

Actors

Ruling families and regimes, national oil companies, citizens, and foreign oil companies and buyers.

Connections

Core/periphery (M2), protectorate (M3), Wahhabism (M4), OPEC and Bretton Woods (this module).

Critiques
  • Oil states vary: Kuwait had one of the region's most active elected parliaments for decades, though the emir suspended it in May 2024.
  • Rentier states have faced revolutions and uprisings: Iran in 1979, Bahrain and Libya in 2011.
  • Many non-oil Arab states are also authoritarian, so oil cannot be the only cause.
  • Institutions that existed before oil, and the timing of oil discovery, shape outcomes.
Today
outside context Saudi Arabia introduced a 5% value-added tax in 2018 and tripled it to 15% in 2020. Whether new taxes create new demands for representation is a live test of rentier theory.
Model ID

A rentier state draws a large share of its revenue from external rents, mainly oil and gas, rather than from taxing its population, a concept developed by Hossein Mahdavy and by Beblawi and Luciani. Without taxation, citizens have less leverage, and the state can buy loyalty with jobs and subsidies and fund large security forces. Saudi Arabia and the other Gulf monarchies are the classic cases, while Egypt and Jordan are semi-rentier states relying on canal fees, aid, and remittances. The theory helps explain authoritarian durability in the Gulf. But critics note that Kuwait sustained an active parliament for decades before its 2024 suspension, that rentier states like Iran in 1979 still faced revolution, and that many non-oil states are also authoritarian.

OPEC

Thread: the world economy
What happened
  • The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries was founded in Baghdad in September 1960 by Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela, after the major Western oil companies unilaterally cut the posted price of oil.
  • It was weak in the 1960s. Power shifted in the early 1970s: Libya forced price increases in 1970, producers won the Tehran and Tripoli agreements in 1971, and governments nationalized or took stakes in oil companies, such as Iraq in 1972 and the gradual Saudi takeover of Aramco, completed in 1980.
  • 1973: during the October War, Arab producers organized through OAPEC (the Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries, founded 1968) cut production and embargoed the U.S. and the Netherlands. OPEC's Gulf members raised posted prices in October 1973 and again in January 1974, roughly quadrupling them.
  • A second shock followed the Iranian revolution in 1979. A glut in the 1980s led to a price collapse in 1986.
  • Since 2016 OPEC has coordinated with Russia and others as OPEC+.
Exam precision

The 1973 embargo was an OAPEC action by Arab producers; the price increases were set by OPEC members. Iran, a non-Arab OPEC member, did not join the embargo.

Actors

Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Venezuela, Libya, the major Western oil companies, and consuming countries.

Connections

Core/periphery (M2), imperialism (M3), Bretton Woods, rentier state (this module).

Interpretations
  • Powerful cartel that shifted wealth and power from consumers to producers.
  • Weak cartel: members often cheat on quotas, and outcomes depend mostly on Saudi Arabia's choices and market conditions.
  • Many economists argue the 1973 price rise owed as much to tight markets, nationalizations, and dollar weakness as to the embargo.
Today
outside context Qatar left OPEC in 2019, and OPEC+ decisions with Russia now set much of the market. Producer cooperation persists but depends on members' changing interests.
Model ID

OPEC was founded in Baghdad in 1960 by Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela to resist price cuts by the major Western oil companies. In the early 1970s, producers gained control over prices and production through new agreements and nationalizations. During the 1973 war, Arab producers in OAPEC imposed an embargo, while OPEC's Gulf members roughly quadrupled posted prices in 1973 and 1974. This transferred enormous wealth to producer states and deepened the rentier state model in the Gulf. Analysts debate how powerful OPEC really is, since outcomes often depend on Saudi decisions and market conditions.

Neoliberalism vs. Neoconservatism

Thread: the world economy
Neoliberalism

An economic doctrine favoring markets: privatization, deregulation, trade liberalization, subsidy cuts, and fiscal austerity. outside context Economist John Williamson's 1989 term Washington Consensus describes the policy package promoted by the IMF and World Bank.

  • Egypt: Sadat's infitah (opening) from 1974; IMF-backed subsidy cuts triggered bread riots in January 1977 and were reversed; a fuller structural adjustment program began in 1991.
  • Jordan (1989) and Tunisia (1984) also saw riots after price increases tied to economic reform.
  • Results: growth in some places, but also privatization that enriched regime-connected businessmen, rising inequality, and youth unemployment. outside context Many scholars link these grievances to the 2011 uprisings, though the weight of economics is debated.
Neoconservatism

A U.S. foreign policy outlook holding that America should use its power, including military force, to spread democracy and confront threats, and that tyranny abroad endangers U.S. security.

  • In 1998 a group associated with the Project for the New American Century urged the removal of Saddam Hussein.
  • After the attacks of September 11, 2001, the Bush administration's 2002 National Security Strategy endorsed preemptive action against emerging threats.
  • The 2003 invasion of Iraq, followed by the occupation, de-Ba'athification, insurgency, and sectarian civil war in 2006 to 2007, and a rise in Iranian influence.
  • The Freedom Agenda pushed elections, but Hamas's 2006 victory made democracy promotion awkward for Washington.
Compare
  • Domain: neoliberalism is economic policy; neoconservatism is security and foreign policy.
  • Similarity: both are U.S.-associated, universalist doctrines claiming that their model, markets or democracy, fits everywhere, and both provoked backlash in the region.
  • Tension: neoliberal reforms were often carried out by the very autocrats the U.S. backed, which contradicted the democracy agenda.
Actors

The IMF and World Bank, Sadat and Mubarak, Middle Eastern technocrats, and U.S. officials such as Paul Wolfowitz and the Bush administration.

Connections

Core/periphery (M2), imperialism (M3), Ba'athism, Bretton Woods, rentier state (this module).

Interpretations
  • On the Iraq war, explanations include democratic idealism, fear of weapons of mass destruction, oil and strategic interest, and a post-9/11 desire to reshape the region.
  • On neoliberalism, defenders blame poor implementation and cronyism; critics blame the model.
Today
outside context U.S. policy has moved from democracy promotion toward transactional deals such as the Abraham Accords of 2020 and 2021 and major arms sales to Gulf states.
Model ID

Neoliberalism is an economic doctrine of markets, privatization, subsidy cuts, and austerity, promoted in the Middle East by the IMF and World Bank, while neoconservatism is a U.S. foreign policy outlook that favors using American power, including force, to spread democracy. Neoliberal reform began with Egypt's infitah in 1974 and produced bread riots in 1977, followed by structural adjustment in the 1990s. Neoconservatism shaped the 2003 invasion of Iraq, which led to de-Ba'athification, insurgency, and sectarian civil war. Both were universalist U.S.-associated doctrines that provoked backlash, but they operate in different domains, economics versus security. They also conflicted, since autocrats the U.S. supported carried out neoliberal reforms that contradicted democracy promotion.

Synthesis

How these terms connect, 1940s to 2000s

After independence, the states built under the mandates were taken over by officers and parties promising unity, socialism, and liberation. Nasser in Egypt and the Ba'ath in Syria and Iraq expanded the state through land reform, nationalization, and public employment, a later form of the state-building seen in Modules 3 and 5. Pan-Arabism gave them a shared language, but rivalry among them and the defeat of 1967 broke its promise. The PLO grew out of that failure, as Palestinians took control of their own cause.

Oil reshaped the region's political economy. OPEC, the end of Bretton Woods' fixed dollar, and the 1973 crisis transferred enormous wealth to producer states, making the Gulf monarchies rentier states that could buy loyalty without taxing their citizens. Non-oil states depended on aid, remittances, and eventually IMF loans, which brought neoliberal reform and its social costs.

By the 1980s secular nationalism was in retreat. The Muslim Brotherhood's offshoot, Hamas, challenged the PLO from 1987, and the U.S. turned from economic reform to military democracy promotion with the 2003 invasion of Iraq, which ended Ba'athist rule there and unleashed sectarian conflict.

Mandates → independence → officers' regimes

Weak postcolonial governments fell to coups in Egypt (1952), Syria (1963), and Iraq (1958, then the Ba'ath in 1963 and 1968).

Nasser → pan-Arabism → UAR

Suez 1956 made Nasser its leader; the union with Syria was its peak and its failure.

1967 defeat → PLO takeover and rise of Islamism

The defeat discredited Arab regimes, let Fatah take the PLO, and opened space for Islamist alternatives.

Bretton Woods collapse → OPEC power → rentier states

A weak dollar and nationalizations helped producers seize pricing power, and the 1973 boom deepened rentier rule.

Petrodollars and debt → IMF → neoliberalism

Recycled oil money and later debt brought structural adjustment to non-oil states like Egypt.

Muslim Brotherhood → Hamas → challenge to PLO

Islamism replaced secular nationalism as the main opposition current in Palestinian politics.

Neoconservatism → Iraq 2003 → end of Ba'athism and sectarian conflict

De-Ba'athification removed the old state's backbone and helped fuel insurgency.

One-sentence takeaway for essays

From the 1940s to the 2000s, the region's states were built by officers and parties, financed increasingly by oil and external rents, and shaped by a U.S.-led economic and security order, while the failures of secular nationalism opened the way for Islamist alternatives.
