Module 2 · c. 1500 to 1800

The early modern world

Two stories run side by side. In the Middle East, large gunpowder empires rule. In Europe, religious upheaval, a commercial boom, and a new rule of sovereign states build the power that will press on the region in the 19th century. Sample essay 2 asks directly about the European half, so this module matters more than its age suggests.

Ottoman Empire

Thread: the modern state
What happened

Founded around 1300 by Osman in northwest Anatolia, the empire took Constantinople in 1453 under Mehmed II, conquered Syria and Egypt in 1516 to 1517 under Selim I, and reached its height under Suleiman (r. 1520 to 1566), who besieged Vienna in 1529. It lasted until the sultanate was abolished in 1922.

How it worked
  • Sultan and household: a dynastic ruler served by slave-origin officials and soldiers recruited through the devshirme, including the Janissary infantry.
  • Timar system: cavalrymen received the revenue of a piece of land in exchange for military service, which tied provincial administration to the army.
  • Religious communities: non-Muslim communities ran much of their own religious and personal-status law under their own leaders, an arrangement later called the millet system.
  • Tax farming: as cash needs grew, the state sold the right to collect taxes, including life-term tax farms from 1695.
Why it changed

Gunpowder warfare made infantry with firearms more valuable than timar cavalry. The state needed cash to pay standing troops, so it shifted from timars to tax farming. That move empowered provincial notables (ayan) who held the tax farms, and central control loosened in the 17th and 18th centuries.

Actors

Sultans, the Janissaries, provincial notables, the ulama, tax farmers, and European rivals such as the Habsburgs and Russia.

Connections

Ulama (M1), capitulations and defensive developmentalism (M3), Tanzimat and the Land Code (M3), Ataturk (M5).

Interpretations
  • Decline thesis: after Suleiman the empire decayed steadily until its collapse. Older scholarship held this view.
  • Transformation thesis: the empire adapted. Decentralization through tax farming was a rational response to new military and fiscal pressures, and the empire survived for three more centuries. Most current historians favor this view.
  • The fiscal-military reading links the two: the same military pressures that built strong states in Europe forced the Ottomans to restructure.
Today
outside context Turkish leaders since the 2000s have used Ottoman history to justify a more active regional role, sometimes called neo-Ottomanism. The past is used as a resource; it does not set the policy.
Model ID

The Ottoman Empire was a Sunni dynastic empire founded around 1300 that took Constantinople in 1453 and ruled most of the Arab world after conquering Syria and Egypt in 1516 to 1517. It governed through a sultan's household, Janissary infantry, the timar system of land revenue for cavalry service, and self-governing religious communities. Gunpowder warfare pushed the state toward cash taxation through tax farming, which strengthened provincial notables. Historians now describe this as transformation rather than simple decline. The empire's institutions and borders shaped the states that emerged after its end in 1922.

Safavid and Qajar empires

Thread: the modern state
What happened

Safavids (1501 to 1722): Ismail I founded the dynasty with the support of Turkic tribal fighters, the Qizilbash, and made Twelver Shi'ism the state religion. Abbas I (r. 1588 to 1629) built a capital at Isfahan and created slave-origin regiments of Georgian and other converts to balance the tribes. Afghan invaders took Isfahan in 1722.

Qajars (1789 to 1925): a Turkic tribal dynasty that reunified Iran after decades of fragmentation and ruled from Tehran. The state was weak, relying on tribes, local notables, and the ulama rather than a large bureaucracy.

Why it mattered
  • The Safavids created the Shi'i core of modern Iran.
  • The Qajars lost the Caucasus to Russia in the treaties of Gulistan (1813) and Turkmenchay (1828). Turkmenchay also gave Russian subjects legal privileges similar to capitulations.
  • Short of money, the Qajars sold concessions to Europeans, such as the Reuter concession (1872) and the tobacco concession (1890), which provoked the Tobacco Protest.
Actors

Safavid shahs, Qizilbash tribes, Qajar shahs, the ulama, Russia, and Britain.

Connections

Sunni/Shi'i and ulama (M1), the Great Game and constitutionalism (M3), Reza Shah (M5).

Interpretations

Compared with the Ottomans, Iran's rulers had a weaker central bureaucracy and depended more on tribes and religious scholars. One view treats this as failure. Another treats it as a different but workable model of rule through intermediaries. Either way, the weak Qajar state explains why Iranian reform lagged and why the ulama kept independent power.

Today
outside context Iran's suspicion of foreign economic concessions runs from the Tobacco Protest through the 1951 nationalization of British-controlled oil. History gives later leaders a story to draw on, not a script.
Model ID

The Safavids (1501 to 1722) founded modern Iran's Shi'i identity by making Twelver Shi'ism the state religion and built a capital at Isfahan under Abbas I. The Qajars (1789 to 1925) reunified Iran but ruled a weak state that relied on tribes, notables, and the ulama. The Qajars lost the Caucasus to Russia in 1813 and 1828 and sold concessions to Europeans, such as the 1890 tobacco concession. Compared with the Ottomans, Iran's rulers had less bureaucratic capacity for defensive reform. That weakness left the Iranian ulama independent and helps explain the mass constitutional revolution of 1905 to 1906.

The Protestant Reformation

Thread: sovereignty and the state system
What happened

Martin Luther's challenge to the Catholic Church in 1517 split Latin Christendom. Rulers chose sides, and the Peace of Augsburg (1555) let German princes set the religion of their territories. Religious wars followed, culminating in the Thirty Years' War (1618 to 1648).

Effects on the Middle East
  • Sovereign territorial states: the Reformation broke the universal authority of the pope and emperor. Rulers gained control over churches within their borders. That produced the model of the sovereign territorial state, which Westphalia confirmed and Europe later exported.
  • State-building through war: generations of religious and dynastic war pushed European states to build armies, tax systems, and bureaucracies. That gave Europe the military and administrative power it brought to bear on the Middle East in the 19th century.
  • Short-term Ottoman advantage: outside context a divided Christendom helped the Ottomans. They allied with France against the Habsburgs in the 1530s, and Habsburg troubles with Protestants limited Habsburg pressure on the Ottoman frontier.
Actors

Luther and other reformers, German princes, the Habsburgs, the papacy, and France.

Connections

Peace of Westphalia and Commercial Revolution (this module), the Eastern Question (M3), the mandate system and the nation-state (M5).

Interpretations
  • Institutional and military: the Reformation mattered for the Middle East because it produced sovereign states and the competition that made them powerful. This is the strongest link for the exam.
  • Cultural and economic outside context Max Weber argued that a Protestant ethic encouraged capitalism. The thesis is heavily disputed and is a weaker way to connect the Reformation to the region.
  • Differentiation: the Reformation began a long process in which European states took over functions once held by the church. Secularism in this sense means differentiation of spheres, with the state absorbing roles, not the disappearance of religion. The same process later happened in Middle Eastern states.
Today

Middle Eastern states are sovereign territorial states built on a model that began in Reformation-era Europe. Debates over the role of religion in those states are debates over how far the state should absorb religious functions.

Model ID

The Protestant Reformation began with Martin Luther's challenge to the Catholic Church in 1517 and split Latin Christendom, leading to religious wars that ended with the Peace of Westphalia in 1648. It broke the universal authority of pope and emperor and gave rulers control over religion within their borders, which produced the sovereign territorial state. Constant war pushed European states to build armies, tax systems, and bureaucracies. Its main effect on the Middle East was indirect: it created the kind of state that later overpowered the region and became the only legitimate political unit after World War I. In the short term, a divided Christendom helped the Ottomans, who allied with France against the Habsburgs.

The Commercial Revolution (Europe)

Thread: the world economy
What happened

From roughly 1500 to 1700, European commerce expanded dramatically. Portugal reached India around Africa in 1498. Silver from the Americas flowed into Europe. New institutions spread: joint-stock companies such as the English East India Company (1600) and the Dutch East India Company (1602), along with banking, insurance, and state policies to promote trade.

Effects on the Middle East
  • Trade routes: the Cape route let Europeans reach Asian goods without Middle Eastern intermediaries. outside context Historians debate how fast this hurt the region, since the Levant spice trade recovered for part of the 16th century.
  • Silver and prices: outside context American silver contributed to inflation in the Ottoman Empire in the late 16th century, which hurt people on fixed incomes, including timar holders and soldiers. Historians debate how large this effect was.
  • Capital and organization: European merchants gained capital and corporate tools that let them dominate long-distance trade. This set up the core and periphery relationship of the 19th century.
Actors

European trading companies, merchants and bankers, European states, and Ottoman and Safavid rulers who granted trading rights.

Connections

Core/periphery (this module), capitulations (M3), Bretton Woods and oil (M6).

Interpretations
  • World-systems view: the Commercial Revolution began the European-centered world economy, in which the Middle East would become a periphery.
  • Internal institutions view: outside context Timur Kuran argues that features of Islamic commercial law, such as partnership and inheritance rules, made it hard to build large, lasting firms, so local merchants could not match European companies. This shifts weight from European power to internal institutions.
  • A strong answer combines them: external change created the opportunity, and institutions shaped who could take it.
Today
outside context Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping from late 2023 pushed many ships to sail around Africa rather than through the Suez Canal, the same route Portugal opened in 1498. The region's place in world trade still depends on choke points that others can bypass.
Model ID

The Commercial Revolution was the expansion of European trade and financial institutions from about 1500 to 1700, including the sea route around Africa in 1498, American silver, and joint-stock companies such as the Dutch East India Company of 1602. It allowed Europeans to bypass Middle Eastern intermediaries in the Asian trade, though the timing of that loss is debated. American silver contributed to Ottoman inflation in the late 16th century. Most importantly, European merchants gained capital and corporate organization that let them dominate trade. This laid the groundwork for the core and periphery relationship that pulled the Middle East into a European-centered economy.

Peace of Westphalia

Thread: sovereignty and the state system
What happened

The treaties of Osnabruck and Munster in 1648 ended the Thirty Years' War in the Holy Roman Empire and the Eighty Years' War between Spain and the Dutch Republic. They confirmed that rulers controlled religion in their territories and recognized the independence of the Dutch Republic and the Swiss Confederation.

Principles associated with it
  • Territorial sovereignty: each state has final authority within its borders.
  • Non-interference in other states' internal affairs.
  • Legal equality of sovereign states, regardless of size.
Why it matters for the Middle East
  • The Ottoman Empire, Iran, and the caliphate rested on different ideas of authority: dynastic, imperial, and religious claims that did not depend on fixed borders.
  • Over the 19th century the Ottomans were drawn into European diplomacy. In 1856 the Treaty of Paris formally admitted the Ottoman Empire into the European system of public law.
  • After World War I the territorial nation-state became the only legitimate unit, and the region was divided into states with fixed borders (M5).
Actors

European monarchs and diplomats in 1648; later, Ottoman diplomats and the powers that drew postwar borders.

Connections

Reformation (this module), the Eastern Question (M3), Sykes-Picot and mandates (M5), pan-Arabism (M6).

Interpretations
  • outside context Some scholars argue that Westphalian sovereignty is partly a myth: the treaties did not create modern sovereignty all at once, and the idea was built up over the following centuries. For the exam, treat Westphalia as the symbol of the sovereign-state model rather than a single moment of creation.
  • A second debate asks whether the state system was imposed on the region or adopted by local elites. Both happened: Ottoman reformers adopted it, while Britain and France imposed its postwar borders.
Today
outside context In 2014 the Islamic State staged the destruction of a border marker between Syria and Iraq and declared the end of Sykes-Picot, rejecting the territorial state. The borders survived, which shows how entrenched the state system has become.
Model ID

The Peace of Westphalia (1648) ended the Thirty Years' War and came to symbolize the system of sovereign territorial states: final authority within borders, non-interference, and legal equality among states. This model differed from the dynastic, imperial, and religious authority of the Ottoman Empire, Iran, and the caliphate. Over the 19th century the Ottomans were drawn into the European state system, formally entering it at the Treaty of Paris in 1856. After World War I the territorial nation-state became the only legitimate political unit, and the region was divided into bordered states. Westphalia matters for the Middle East as the origin of the state system the region was absorbed into.

Economic Core/Periphery

Thread: the world economy
What it means
outside context A framework from world-systems theory, associated with Immanuel Wallerstein. The core has high-value manufacturing, capital, and strong states. The periphery exports raw materials, has weaker states, and depends on prices and credit set in the core. A semi-periphery sits between them.
How it applied
  • The Middle East was integrated into the world economy as a periphery, especially in the 19th century.
  • Egypt: long-staple cotton, introduced in the 1820s, became the main export. The American Civil War (1861 to 1865) caused a cotton boom, followed by a bust.
  • Mount Lebanon: raw silk for French mills in Lyon.
  • Anatolia and Iraq: grain, dried fruit, and dates for export.
Why it mattered
  • Dependence on a few exports exposed economies to price swings.
  • Governments borrowed heavily from European banks. Egypt went bankrupt in 1876 and accepted European control of its finances; the Ottoman state defaulted in 1875, and Europeans took over part of its revenue through the Ottoman Public Debt Administration in 1881.
  • New classes rose: merchants (often non-Muslims under foreign protection) and large landowners producing for export. Port cities such as Beirut, Alexandria, and Izmir boomed.
Actors

European banks and merchants, Middle Eastern rulers who borrowed, local merchants and landowners, and peasants who grew export crops.

Connections

Commercial Revolution (this module), capitulations, the Land Code, and imperialism (M3), rentier state (M6).

Interpretations
  • Dependency and world-systems: peripheral integration locked the region into unequal exchange and debt.
  • Modernization: outside context integration brought growth, infrastructure, and new wealth; the problem was policy, not trade itself.
  • Internal institutions: outside context explanations like Kuran's stress local legal and economic institutions over the structure of the world economy.
Today
outside context Gulf oil states still earn most of their revenue from one primary commodity. Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030 diversification plan is an attempt to escape that position, which shows that peripheral status can be addressed by policy rather than being fixed.
Model ID

Core and periphery is a world-systems framework in which the core produces high-value manufactured goods and the periphery exports raw materials and depends on capital from the core. The Middle East was integrated as a periphery in the 19th century, exporting goods such as Egyptian cotton and Lebanese silk. Egypt's cotton boom during the American Civil War was followed by a bust and heavy borrowing. Egypt's bankruptcy in 1876 and the Ottoman default in 1875 led to European control over state finances. Peripheral integration therefore created the debt and dependence that opened the door to European political control.

Synthesis

How these terms connect, c. 1500 to 1800

Between 1500 and 1800, the Middle East and Europe were on different paths that later collided. The Ottoman Empire and Safavid Iran were among the strongest states in the world in the 16th century. Meanwhile, the Reformation broke Europe's religious unity, and the wars that followed forced European rulers to build stronger armies, tax systems, and bureaucracies. Westphalia in 1648 symbolized the result: a system of sovereign territorial states competing constantly.

At the same time, the Commercial Revolution gave European merchants new routes, silver, and companies. The Middle East lost some of its role as the middleman in Asian trade, and European capital grew. By the 19th century these changes produced a world economy with Europe at the core and the Middle East moving toward the periphery.

The Ottoman and Iranian responses differed. The Ottomans restructured, moving from timars to tax farming to raise cash. Iran's Safavid state collapsed in 1722, and the Qajars rebuilt a weaker state. That difference in state capacity shaped how each could respond to Europe in Module 3.

Reformation → Westphalia → state system

Religious division and war produced the sovereign territorial state, which became the template later applied to the Middle East.

Reformation and war → European military power

Constant competition built European fiscal-military states that would outmatch the Ottomans and Qajars by the 19th century.

Commercial Revolution → core and periphery

New routes, silver, and companies shifted economic power to Europe and set up the region's peripheral role.

Military change → Ottoman transformation

Gunpowder warfare made timar cavalry obsolete, pushing the Ottomans toward tax farming and empowering provincial notables.

Safavid Shi'ism → Ottoman rivalry and Iranian identity

The 1501 decision created a religious frontier and the Shi'i core of modern Iran.

Qajar weakness → independent ulama

A weak state could not absorb the clergy, which matters for Iranian politics in Module 3 and beyond.

One-sentence takeaway for essays

The early modern period did not doom the Middle East, but it built the two systems that would reshape it: a European state system born of religious war and Westphalia, and a European-centered world economy born of the Commercial Revolution.
