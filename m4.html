Module 4 · 18th to 20th century

Ideas and movements

As empires weakened and states expanded, people offered new answers to two questions: who is the political community, and what role should Islam play in public life? Nationalism and Zionism answer the first. Wahhabism, Salafism, and the Muslim Brotherhood answer the second. Pogroms and aliyah show how events outside the region fed directly into it.

Nationalism

Thread: identity and nationalism
What it means

The idea that a nation, a people defined by shared language, ethnicity, history, religion, or territory, should govern itself in its own state, and that political legitimacy comes from the nation rather than from a dynasty or religion.

How it arrived
  • Balkan Christians first: Serbian revolts from 1804 and Greek independence, recognized in 1830.
  • Armenian political movements from the 1880s.
  • Turkish nationalism among the Young Turks, articulated by thinkers such as Ziya Gokalp, especially after the Balkan Wars of 1912 to 1913.
  • Arab nationalism: a cultural revival of the Arabic language and literature (the Nahda), then small political societies such as al-Fatat (founded 1909) and the Arab Congress in Paris in 1913.
  • Egyptian nationalism focused on Egypt as a territory, culminating in the 1919 revolution led by the Wafd.
  • Zionism as Jewish nationalism (below).
Why it happened

State schools, print, newspapers, and new armies spread shared languages and identities. Imperial decline and European examples made national independence seem possible. After World War I, the nation-state became the only legitimate political unit.

Actors

Intellectuals and journalists, army officers, students, the Young Turks, Arab societies, and later the mandate powers.

Connections

Osmanlilik (M3), Zionism (this module), Arab Revolt, mandate, Kemalism (M5), pan-Arabism and Ba'athism (M6).

Interpretations
  • Primordialist: nations are ancient, and nationalism awakens them.
  • Modernist: outside context scholars such as Ernest Gellner and Benedict Anderson argue that nations are modern creations of industrialization, mass education, and print. The course's attention to state institutions fits this view.
  • The Arab case: outside context many historians argue that Arab nationalism was weak before World War I and that most Arabs stayed loyal to the Ottomans; it became a mass force only after the war, under the mandates.
Today
outside context The Kurds are often called the largest nation without a state. Iraqi Kurdistan's 2017 independence referendum passed overwhelmingly but was reversed by Baghdad and opposed by neighbors. Nationalism's demand for a state still collides with the borders set after World War I.
Model ID

Nationalism is the idea that a nation defined by shared language, ethnicity, history, or territory should have its own state, with legitimacy flowing from the nation rather than from a dynasty or religion. It reached the Middle East in the 19th century through Balkan Christians such as the Greeks, whose independence was recognized in 1830, and later through Turkish, Arab, Egyptian, and Jewish nationalisms. Modernist scholars tie it to state schools, print, and mass armies rather than ancient identities. Arab nationalism was limited before World War I and grew mainly under the mandates. After the war, the nation-state became the only legitimate unit in the region, which is why competing nationalisms shaped borders and conflicts.

Wahhabism

Thread: religion and politics
What happened

An 18th century reform movement founded by Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab (1703 to 1792) in Najd, central Arabia. It stressed strict monotheism (tawhid) and condemned practices it saw as associating others with God, such as venerating saints, visiting shrines, and many Sufi and Shi'i practices.

The alliance
  • In 1744 Ibn Abd al-Wahhab allied with Muhammad ibn Saud, ruler of Diriyah. The movement gave religious legitimacy to Saudi expansion, and the Saudis gave the movement political protection.
  • The first Saudi state was destroyed in 1818 by Muhammad Ali's Egyptian army acting for the Ottoman sultan.
  • A second Saudi state rose in the 19th century.
  • Abd al-Aziz ibn Saud captured Riyadh in 1902, conquered the Hijaz in 1924 to 1925, and proclaimed the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia in 1932.
Why it happened

Religious revival among scholars combined with the needs of state-building in a region of competing tribes. A shared, strict creed helped unify tribes under one ruler.

Actors

Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab and his descendants (the Al al-Shaykh family), the Saud dynasty, tribal fighters, and the Ottoman and Egyptian forces that opposed them.

Connections

Salafism and the Muslim Brotherhood (this module), Islamism (M1), defensive developmentalism (M3), OPEC and the rentier state (M6).

Interpretations
  • Religious revival: a sincere movement to purify practice.
  • State-building ideology: religion as the glue that let one family unify Arabia.
  • Origin of jihadism: outside context some writers trace modern jihadist groups to Wahhabism; others argue this overstates the link and ignores the Saudi establishment's loyalty to the state.
  • Note: followers usually reject the label Wahhabi and call themselves Salafis or muwahhidun (monotheists).
Today
outside context Since the late 2010s, under Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, Saudi Arabia has curbed the religious police and clerical influence while promoting a more moderate image of Islam. The Saudi state decides how much power its religious establishment has, which reverses the image of Wahhabism controlling the state.
Model ID

Wahhabism is an 18th century reform movement founded by Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab in central Arabia that called for strict monotheism and condemned saint veneration, shrine visits, and many Sufi and Shi'i practices. In 1744 he allied with Muhammad ibn Saud, exchanging religious legitimacy for political protection. That alliance produced the first Saudi state, destroyed by Muhammad Ali's forces in 1818, and later the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, proclaimed in 1932. Wahhabism differs from 19th century Salafi modernism, which sought to reconcile Islam with modern reason and science. Its history shows religion serving as a tool of state-building, with the Saudi state ultimately controlling the religious establishment.

Salafism

Thread: religion and politics
What it means

From al-salaf al-salih, "the pious ancestors," the first generations of Muslims. Salafism is a broad label for movements calling for a return to the practice of the early community. It covers two very different strands.

Strand 1: Salafi modernism
  • Late 19th century reformers: Jamal al-Din al-Afghani (1838 or 1839 to 1897), Muhammad Abduh (1849 to 1905, Grand Mufti of Egypt from 1899), and Rashid Rida (1865 to 1935), who published the journal al-Manar from 1898.
  • They argued that Islam was compatible with reason, science, and constitutional government; blamed Muslim weakness on blind imitation of past rulings (taqlid); and called for fresh interpretation (ijtihad).
  • Their goal was to strengthen Muslim societies against European domination.
Strand 2: purist Salafism
  • In the 20th century the label came to mean literalist movements that often converge with Wahhabism. Rida himself grew sympathetic to the Saudis later in life.
  • outside context Scholars such as Quintan Wiktorowicz divide purist Salafis into quietists who avoid politics, politicos who engage in it, and jihadis who use violence.
Why it happened

European power and the expanding state raised the question of why Muslim societies had fallen behind. Modernists answered that Islam, properly understood, was not the problem; later purists answered that Muslims had strayed from the original faith.

Actors

Afghani, Abduh, Rida, and later Salafi scholars and movements.

Connections

Ulama and Islamism (M1), constitutionalism (M3), Wahhabism and the Muslim Brotherhood (this module).

Interpretations
  • outside context Historian Henri Lauziere argues that the modernists rarely called themselves Salafis and that the label was applied to them later. For the exam, use "Salafi modernism" for Afghani and Abduh and "purist Salafism" for the later strand, and say why they differ.
Today
outside context In Egypt's 2011 to 2012 parliamentary elections, the Salafi Nour Party won about a quarter of the seats. Purists who once avoided politics entered it when the opportunity arose, which shows strategy responding to circumstance.
Model ID

Salafism refers to movements calling for a return to the practice of the pious ancestors, the first generations of Muslims. In the late 19th century, Salafi modernists such as Jamal al-Din al-Afghani and Muhammad Abduh argued that Islam was compatible with reason, science, and constitutional government and called for fresh interpretation instead of blind imitation. Their goal was to strengthen Muslim societies against European domination. In the 20th century, the label came to describe purist, literalist movements that often converge with Wahhabism and range from quietist to jihadi. The contrast with Wahhabism is that modernist Salafism was an urban response to Europe, while Wahhabism was an 18th century Arabian revival tied to Saudi state-building.

The Muslim Brotherhood

Thread: religion and politics
What happened

Founded in 1928 in Ismailia, Egypt, by Hasan al-Banna, a schoolteacher. Ismailia was the headquarters of the Suez Canal Company, a daily reminder of foreign control.

How it grew
  • A mass organization of preaching, schools, clinics, welfare, youth groups, and, from the late 1930s, a secret armed wing.
  • It grew to hundreds of thousands of members by the late 1940s.
  • Al-Banna was assassinated in 1949.
  • Nasser crushed the organization after a 1954 assassination attempt on him was blamed on the Brotherhood. Sayyid Qutb radicalized in prison, wrote Milestones (1964), and was executed in 1966.
  • Under Sadat and Mubarak it was tolerated at times and ran candidates as independents, winning 88 seats in 2005.
  • outside context After 2011 its Freedom and Justice Party won elections, and Mohamed Morsi became president in 2012. The military removed him in July 2013 and outlawed the Brotherhood.
Why it happened
  • British occupation and foreign economic control.
  • Urbanization and the spread of education, which produced a lower middle class open to new ideas.
  • The failure of liberal parties to win full independence.
  • Strong organization and social services that met real needs.
Actors

Hasan al-Banna, Sayyid Qutb, Nasser, Sadat, Mubarak, Morsi, and branches abroad.

Branches

Offshoots and affiliates include Jordan's Islamic Action Front, the Syrian Brotherhood, crushed at Hama in 1982 by Hafez al-Assad, and Hamas, founded in 1987 (M6).

Connections

Islamism (M1), Salafism (this module), Nasser and Hamas (M6).

Interpretations
  • Moderate and participatory: a movement that accepts elections and works through society.
  • Gateway to radicalism: Qutb's ideas inspired later jihadists.
  • Social services and organization: outside context Tarek Masoud argues that Islamist electoral success came largely from organizational networks and services rather than from voters' religious views.
Today
outside context The Brotherhood divides the region's governments: Qatar and Turkey have been sympathetic, while Egypt, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE treat it as a threat. That split was one cause of the 2017 to 2021 blockade of Qatar.
Model ID

The Muslim Brotherhood was founded in 1928 in Ismailia, Egypt, by schoolteacher Hasan al-Banna as a mass movement for Islamic reform of society and resistance to foreign control. It grew through preaching, schools, clinics, and welfare networks to hundreds of thousands of members by the late 1940s. Nasser crushed it after 1954, and Sayyid Qutb's prison writings radicalized part of the movement. It later contested elections and won Egypt's presidency in 2012, before the military removed Mohamed Morsi in 2013. The Brotherhood is the founding organization of modern Islamism and the parent of affiliates such as Hamas.

Zionism

Thread: identity and nationalism
What it means

The Jewish national movement that sought a Jewish national home, and later a state, in the Land of Israel (Palestine).

What happened
  • Early stage: the Hovevei Zion (Lovers of Zion) societies of the 1880s and Leon Pinsker's pamphlet Auto-Emancipation (1882).
  • Political Zionism: Theodor Herzl published The Jewish State in 1896 and convened the First Zionist Congress in Basel in 1897, which founded the Zionist Organization and called for a legally secured home in Palestine.
  • Institutions: the Jewish National Fund (1901) bought land; later the Jewish Agency represented the community.
  • Strands: political Zionism (diplomacy with great powers), practical Zionism (settlement), cultural Zionism (Ahad Ha'am), Labor Zionism (David Ben-Gurion), and Revisionist Zionism (Ze'ev Jabotinsky, 1920s).
  • Chaim Weizmann's lobbying helped win the Balfour Declaration in 1917 (M5).
Why it happened

European nationalism offered the model; antisemitism and pogroms showed the limits of emancipation and assimilation; the Dreyfus Affair in France (from 1894) affected Herzl; and religious and historical ties pointed to Palestine.

Actors

Herzl, Weizmann, Ben-Gurion, Jabotinsky, Zionist institutions, European governments, and the Arab population of Palestine.

Connections

Nationalism, pogrom, and aliyah (this module), Land Code (M3), Balfour and mandate (M5), PLO and Hamas (M6).

Interpretations
  • National liberation: the movement of a persecuted people seeking safety and self-determination.
  • Settler colonialism: outside context a movement of European settlers that displaced the existing Arab population with the backing of an imperial power.
  • Many historians see features of both: a nationalist movement that operated in the age of empire and relied on imperial sponsorship. Present this as a live debate.
Today
outside context Israel's 2018 Basic Law defined it as the nation-state of the Jewish people, reigniting debate over the status of Arab citizens. Zionism's founding questions about nation, land, and minorities remain politically active.
Model ID

Zionism is the Jewish national movement that sought a national home, and later a state, in Palestine. It drew on European nationalism and responded to antisemitism and pogroms, beginning with the Lovers of Zion in the 1880s and taking political form when Theodor Herzl convened the First Zionist Congress in Basel in 1897. Zionist institutions such as the Jewish National Fund bought land and organized immigration, and Chaim Weizmann's lobbying helped secure the Balfour Declaration in 1917. Supporters see it as national liberation for a persecuted people, while critics analyze it as settler colonialism backed by empire. Its success in building a Jewish community in Palestine under the British mandate set up the Israeli and Palestinian conflict.

Pogrom

Thread: identity and nationalism
What it means

A Russian word for an organized violent attack on a minority, used especially for attacks on Jews in the Russian Empire.

What happened
  • 1881 to 1884: a wave of pogroms after the assassination of Tsar Alexander II, followed by the May Laws of 1882 restricting Jewish life.
  • Kishinev (1903): a notorious pogrom that drew international outrage, followed by a larger wave during the 1905 revolution.
  • 1918 to 1921: pogroms during the Russian Civil War killed tens of thousands of Jews.
Why it mattered
  • Pogroms and discrimination drove mass emigration: roughly two million Jews left Eastern Europe, mostly the Russian Empire, between 1881 and 1914, and most went to the United States.
  • A small share went to Palestine, forming the First Aliyah (1882 to 1903) and, after Kishinev and 1905, the Second Aliyah (1904 to 1914), which brought many future Labor Zionist leaders, including Ben-Gurion in 1906.
  • Pogroms persuaded many that assimilation would not protect Jews, strengthening Zionism.
Actors

Russian mobs and officials, Jewish communities, emigrants, and Zionist organizers.

Connections

Zionism and aliyah (this module), Balfour Declaration (M5).

Interpretations

Historians debate how far the Russian state organized pogroms; many now argue they were mostly local outbreaks that authorities tolerated or failed to stop. Either way, the effect on emigration and Zionism is clear.

Today
outside context The term is used in current politics, for example by an Israeli general describing a 2023 settler attack on the Palestinian town of Huwara, and by many describing the October 7, 2023 attack. Because the word carries heavy historical weight, its use is itself contested.
Model ID

A pogrom is an organized violent attack on a minority, especially the attacks on Jews in the Russian Empire. Major waves came in 1881 to 1884 after the assassination of Alexander II, in 1903 at Kishinev and during the 1905 revolution, and during the Russian Civil War. Pogroms and discriminatory laws helped push about two million Jews to leave Eastern Europe, mostly the Russian Empire, between 1881 and 1914, most for the United States. A small share went to Palestine in the First and Second Aliyah, and pogroms persuaded many that assimilation could not protect Jews. Pogroms thus link European antisemitism directly to Zionism and to demographic change in Palestine.

Aliyah

Thread: identity and nationalism
What it means

Hebrew for "ascent": Jewish immigration to the Land of Israel.

Waves (approximate numbers)
  • First Aliyah (1882 to 1903): about 25,000 to 35,000, mostly from the Russian Empire and Romania; agricultural colonies such as Rishon LeZion (1882), many supported by Baron Edmond de Rothschild.
  • Second Aliyah (1904 to 1914): about 35,000 to 40,000; socialist pioneers, the first kibbutz at Degania (1910), the founding of Tel Aviv (1909), and the revival of Hebrew.
  • Third (1919 to 1923) and Fourth (1924 to 1928): pioneers after World War I, then many middle-class Polish Jews.
  • Fifth (1929 to 1939): about 250,000, many fleeing Nazi Germany after 1933.
  • Britain's 1939 White Paper limited immigration, after which illegal immigration (Aliyah Bet) grew.
Why it mattered
  • The Jewish share of Palestine's population grew from about one-tenth in the early 1920s to about one-third by 1947.
  • Land purchases, often from absentee owners, displaced Arab tenant farmers.
  • Labor Zionists pushed "Hebrew labor," a separate Jewish economy that excluded Arab workers.
  • Arab opposition grew: riots in 1920, 1921, and 1929, and the Arab Revolt of 1936 to 1939.
Actors

Jewish immigrants and Zionist institutions, Arab peasants and notables, and British mandate authorities.

Connections

Land Code (M3), pogrom and Zionism (this module), Balfour and mandate (M5), PLO (M6).

Interpretations

Zionists described aliyah as return and the ingathering of exiles. Palestinian Arabs experienced it as colonization that threatened their majority and land. The same movement of people carries opposite meanings.

Today
outside context Israel's Law of Return (1950) grants Jews worldwide the right to immigrate, while Palestinian refugees' claimed right of return is a central unresolved issue. About one million immigrants from the former Soviet Union in the 1990s also reshaped Israeli politics.
Model ID

Aliyah, Hebrew for ascent, is Jewish immigration to the Land of Israel. It came in waves: the First Aliyah of 1882 to 1903 founded agricultural colonies, the Second of 1904 to 1914 brought socialist pioneers who founded Degania and Tel Aviv, and the Fifth of 1929 to 1939 brought about 250,000 people, many fleeing Nazi Germany. Immigration raised the Jewish share of Palestine's population from about one-tenth in the early 1920s to about one-third by 1947. Land purchases and the push for separate Jewish labor displaced Arab tenants and fueled Arab opposition, including the revolt of 1936 to 1939. Aliyah turned Zionism from an idea into a demographic and political reality.

Synthesis

How these terms connect, 18th to 20th century

This module spans the longest period, and the terms connect through two threads: identity and religion.

On identity: as Osmanlilik faded, nationalism offered the main alternative. Balkan Christians went first, then Turks, Arabs, and Egyptians. In Europe, the pogroms of 1881 and 1903 pushed Jews toward emigration, and Zionism turned a small part of that emigration into organized aliyah to Palestine. By 1914 Palestine had a growing Zionist community, and Arab nationalism was beginning to take shape, mostly among elites.

On religion: Wahhabism arose in 18th century Arabia, before European pressure arrived, as a revival tied to Saudi state-building. Salafi modernism arose in the late 19th century as a direct response to European power and the expanding state, arguing that a purified Islam could be modern. The Muslim Brotherhood, founded in 1928, turned these ideas into a mass political organization aimed at the state itself. That is the start of Islamism as defined in Module 1.

Pogroms → emigration → aliyah

Violence in the Russian Empire after 1881 and 1903 drove emigration; a small share went to Palestine as the First and Second Aliyah.

Nationalism → Zionism

Zionism applied European nationalism to Jews, seeking a state for a diaspora people.

Zionism and aliyah → Arab opposition

Land purchases and Jewish immigration fed Palestinian Arab nationalism and later revolt.

Wahhabism → Saudi state

The 1744 alliance made religion the glue of Saudi expansion, leading to the 1932 kingdom.

European pressure → Salafi modernism

Afghani and Abduh asked why Muslim societies had fallen behind and answered with reform and reason.

Salafi modernism → Muslim Brotherhood

Rida's ideas influenced al-Banna, who built a mass movement for Islamic reform of society and the state.

Expanding state → Islamism

The Brotherhood targeted the modern state, which shows Islamism is a modern phenomenon, not a survival.

One-sentence takeaway for essays

Between the 18th and 20th centuries, empire's decline and the state's expansion produced competing answers to who the community is and what role Islam should play, and those answers, nationalism, Zionism, and Islamism, still organize the region's politics.
