Module 5 · 1914 to 1940s

World War I and the postwar order

World War I destroyed the Ottoman Empire. Britain made three wartime commitments that pointed in different directions, and the victors then built a new state system through the mandates. Where local leaders won their own wars or seized power, as in Turkey and Iran, they built new states by decree. This module is the hinge between the 19th century and the modern Middle East.

The Arab Revolt

Thread: identity and nationalism
What happened
  • In June 1916 Sharif Hussein ibn Ali of Mecca, from the Hashemite family and the Ottoman-appointed guardian of the holy cities, launched a revolt against the Ottomans with British money, arms, and advisers, including T. E. Lawrence.
  • His sons Faisal and Abdullah led the fighting. Arab forces took Aqaba in 1917 and entered Damascus with British forces in October 1918.
  • Faisal set up an Arab government in Damascus, and a Syrian Congress declared him king in March 1920. France defeated his forces at Maysalun in July 1920 and expelled him.
  • Britain then made Faisal king of Iraq (1921) and Abdullah ruler of Transjordan (1921).
  • Ibn Saud conquered the Hijaz from Hussein in 1924 to 1925.
Why it happened
  • Hussein's dynastic ambition and his fear that the Ottoman government would remove him.
  • Ottoman centralization under the Committee of Union and Progress, and Jamal Pasha's wartime repression in Syria, including the execution of Arab activists in 1915 and 1916.
  • British promises in the Hussein-McMahon correspondence and British subsidies.
Actors

Sharif Hussein, Faisal, Abdullah, T. E. Lawrence, the British in Cairo, Jamal Pasha, and France.

Connections

Nationalism (M4), Hussein-McMahon, Sykes-Picot, mandate (this module), pan-Arabism (M6).

Interpretations
  • National awakening: the first armed expression of Arab nationalism.
  • Dynastic and British-sponsored: a Hashemite bid for power, fought mostly by Bedouin tribes paid with British gold, with little support from urban nationalists.
  • Both are partly right: the revolt had limited participation at the time but became a powerful symbol afterward.
Today

Jordan's Hashemite monarchy traces its legitimacy to the revolt, and Jordan's flag is based on the revolt's flag. A dynasty created by the postwar settlement still rules.

Model ID

The Arab Revolt was the uprising against Ottoman rule launched in June 1916 by Sharif Hussein of Mecca with British money, arms, and advisers such as T. E. Lawrence. Led by Hussein's sons Faisal and Abdullah, Arab forces took Aqaba in 1917 and entered Damascus with the British in 1918. It was driven by Hussein's dynastic ambitions, Ottoman wartime repression in Syria, and British promises in the Hussein-McMahon correspondence. France expelled Faisal from Syria in 1920, and Britain made him king of Iraq and Abdullah ruler of Transjordan in 1921. The revolt is debated as a national awakening or a British-backed dynastic bid, but it became a founding symbol of Arab nationalism and of the Jordanian monarchy.

Hussein-McMahon correspondence

Thread: sovereignty and the state system
What happened

An exchange of ten letters between July 1915 and March 1916 between Sharif Hussein and Sir Henry McMahon, the British High Commissioner in Egypt. Britain pledged to recognize and support Arab independence in a large area in return for an Arab revolt against the Ottomans.

The exclusions
  • Britain excluded coastal areas west of the districts of Damascus, Homs, Hama, and Aleppo, arguing they were not purely Arab.
  • It also reserved French interests and special British arrangements in the Baghdad and Basra regions.
  • Whether Palestine was excluded became the central dispute. Britain later said it was; Arab leaders said it was not. The letters never mention Palestine by name.
Why it happened

Britain wanted an Arab uprising to weaken the Ottomans and to counter the Ottoman sultan's call for jihad. Hussein wanted a large Arab kingdom for his family.

Actors

Sharif Hussein, Henry McMahon, and the British government.

Connections

Arab Revolt, Sykes-Picot, Balfour, mandate (this module).

Interpretations
  • Betrayal: Britain promised independence and delivered mandates.
  • Ambiguity: the letters were vague and never a formal treaty, and both sides read them in their own interest.
  • Most historians agree the wording was deliberately vague and that it conflicted, at least in spirit, with Sykes-Picot and Balfour.
Today

The sense of Western betrayal rooted in these letters remains part of Arab political memory and is often invoked in criticism of Western policy.

Model ID

The Hussein-McMahon correspondence was an exchange of letters in 1915 and 1916 in which Britain's High Commissioner in Egypt pledged to support Arab independence in return for Sharif Hussein's revolt against the Ottomans. Britain excluded areas west of Damascus, Homs, Hama, and Aleppo and reserved French and British interests, but the letters never named Palestine, which became the core dispute. It conflicted with Britain's secret Sykes-Picot Agreement with France in 1916 and the Balfour Declaration of 1917. Unlike Sykes-Picot, it was addressed to an Arab leader and promised independence, though in vague terms. The gap between its promise and the postwar mandates became a lasting source of Arab grievance.

Sykes-Picot Agreement

Thread: sovereignty and the state system
What happened
  • A secret agreement concluded in May 1916 between Mark Sykes for Britain and Francois Georges-Picot for France, with Russian consent.
  • France would get direct control of coastal Syria and Lebanon and parts of southeastern Anatolia, and influence over interior Syria, including Mosul.
  • Britain would get direct control of southern Mesopotamia (Baghdad and Basra) and influence over the area between, including Transjordan.
  • Most of Palestine would be under international administration.
  • In the zones of influence, an Arab state or confederation of states could exist under French or British protection.
  • The Bolsheviks published the agreement in late 1917, embarrassing Britain.
What actually happened

Postwar borders differed from the agreement. Mosul went to Britain, Palestine became a British mandate, and the final borders were set by the San Remo conference (1920), later treaties, and boundary commissions.

Why it happened

Britain and France wanted to settle their claims in advance and keep the alliance together during the war.

Actors

Mark Sykes, Francois Georges-Picot, the British and French governments, and Russia.

Connections

Westphalia (M2), Eastern Question (M3), Hussein-McMahon, Balfour, mandate (this module).

Interpretations
  • Symbol of imperial betrayal: secret division of Arab lands while promising independence.
  • Over-credited: many historians stress that Sykes-Picot did not draw the actual borders, which came later and differed in key ways. Its importance is as a symbol and as the start of a process of division.
Today
outside context In 2014 the Islamic State announced the end of Sykes-Picot while bulldozing a border berm between Syria and Iraq. The phrase remains shorthand for imposed borders, even though the actual borders came later.
Model ID

The Sykes-Picot Agreement was a secret Anglo-French deal of May 1916, with Russian consent, that divided the Ottoman Arab provinces into zones of direct control and influence. France was assigned coastal Syria and Lebanon and influence over interior Syria, Britain was assigned southern Mesopotamia and influence over the area to its west, and Palestine was to be international. It conflicted with the Hussein-McMahon promises of Arab independence and was published by the Bolsheviks in 1917. The actual postwar borders differed, being set at San Remo in 1920 and in later treaties. Sykes-Picot matters most as a symbol of imposed, secret division that still shapes Arab views of Western power.

Balfour Declaration

Thread: sovereignty and the state system
What happened

A letter of November 2, 1917, from British Foreign Secretary Arthur Balfour to Lord Rothschild, declaring that Britain favored the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people. It added a proviso that nothing should prejudice the civil and religious rights of the existing non-Jewish communities or the rights of Jews in other countries.

Why it happened
  • To win Jewish support for the war effort in the United States and Russia, based partly on exaggerated beliefs about Jewish influence.
  • To secure a British-controlled Palestine near the Suez Canal.
  • Sympathy among some British leaders, including Christian restorationist ideas.
  • Chaim Weizmann's persistent lobbying.
The key tension

Arabs were about nine-tenths of Palestine's population, yet the declaration called them only the existing non-Jewish communities and promised them civil and religious rights, not national or political rights.

Actors

Arthur Balfour, Lord Rothschild, Chaim Weizmann, the British cabinet, and the Arab population of Palestine.

Connections

Zionism and aliyah (M4), Hussein-McMahon and mandate (this module), PLO (M6).

Interpretations
  • Strategic calculation: wartime imperial interest.
  • Idealism: sympathy for Jewish national aspirations.
  • Most historians stress the mix, and the fact that it was incorporated into the League of Nations mandate for Palestine in 1922 gave it legal force.
Today
outside context Palestinians mark the anniversary as the beginning of their dispossession, and Britain's centenary commemoration in 2017 drew protests. The declaration remains a reference point for both national narratives.
Model ID

The Balfour Declaration was a British letter of November 2, 1917, from Foreign Secretary Arthur Balfour to Lord Rothschild, stating support for a national home for the Jewish people in Palestine while protecting the civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities. Britain issued it to win wartime Jewish support, to secure Palestine near the Suez Canal, and in response to Chaim Weizmann's lobbying. It described the Arab majority, about nine-tenths of the population, only as non-Jewish communities and promised them no political rights. It conflicted with the spirit of the Hussein-McMahon correspondence. Its incorporation into the 1922 Palestine mandate gave Zionism international legal backing and set the stage for the conflict.

Mandate

Thread: sovereignty and the state system
What it means

A system created by Article 22 of the League of Nations Covenant (1919) for former Ottoman and German territories. Advanced nations were to administer peoples judged not yet able to govern themselves. The former Ottoman Arab lands were Class A mandates, provisionally recognized as independent nations subject to administrative advice and assistance.

Who got what
  • Allocated at the San Remo conference in April 1920 and confirmed by the League in 1922.
  • Britain: Iraq (mandate ended in 1932 when Iraq joined the League), Palestine, and Transjordan, separated from Palestine's Zionist provisions in 1921 to 1922.
  • France: Syria and Lebanon. France created Greater Lebanon in 1920; both became independent in the 1940s.
How mandates were run
  • France divided Syria into separate states, including an Alawite state and a Druze state, and recruited minorities into its local troops.
  • Britain ruled Iraq through the Hashemite monarchy and Sunni Arab elites.
  • Revolts broke out: Iraq in 1920, the Great Syrian Revolt of 1925 to 1927, and the Arab Revolt in Palestine of 1936 to 1939.
Why it happened

The victors wanted control, but Wilsonian self-determination made open annexation hard to justify. The mandate system reconciled imperial interest with the new language of self-determination.

Actors

The League of Nations, Britain, France, local elites, and revolt leaders.

Connections

Westphalia (M2), protectorate and colonialism (M3), nationalism and Zionism (M4), Sykes-Picot and Balfour (this module), Ba'athism (M6).

Interpretations
  • Colonialism in new clothes: imperial rule with a new label.
  • Real novelty: outside context historian Susan Pedersen argues that League oversight created an international arena where mandatory powers had to justify their rule, which helped make independence the expected end point.
  • Course frame: mandates imposed state-building and made the nation-state the only legitimate unit.
Today
outside context Alawite dominance in Syria's officer corps under the Assads, which ended with the fall of Bashar al-Assad in December 2024, has roots partly in French recruitment of minorities. Mandate-era choices shaped later politics without determining them.
Model ID

A mandate was a territory administered by a European power under Article 22 of the League of Nations Covenant, which treated former Ottoman Arab lands as provisionally independent nations needing temporary guidance. The San Remo conference of 1920 gave Britain Iraq, Palestine, and Transjordan and gave France Syria and Lebanon. Mandatory powers built state institutions while dividing and ruling, as when France split Syria into separate states and recruited minorities into its forces. Mandates differed from protectorates and colonies because they were supervised by the League and supposed to end in independence. They created most of the bordered states of the Arab Middle East and made the nation-state the only legitimate political unit.

Mustafa Kemal (Ataturk)

Thread: the modern state
What happened
  • An Ottoman officer (1881 to 1938) who became famous defending Gallipoli in 1915.
  • After the Ottoman surrender at Mudros in October 1918, the Allies occupied parts of the empire. The Treaty of Sevres (1920) would have partitioned Anatolia among Greeks, Armenians, Kurds, and European zones.
  • Kemal organized national resistance from Anatolia beginning in May 1919, and a Grand National Assembly met in Ankara in April 1920.
  • He won the War of Independence, defeating Greece in 1922.
  • The Treaty of Lausanne (1923) replaced Sevres, recognized Turkey's borders, and ended the capitulations. A Greek-Turkish population exchange moved about 1.2 million Orthodox Christians and about 400,000 Muslims.
  • The sultanate was abolished in 1922, the Republic proclaimed in October 1923, and the caliphate abolished in 1924. In 1934 he took the surname Ataturk, "father of the Turks."
Why it happened

Allied partition plans and the Greek invasion created a national cause, and Kemal, a successful commander, organized the army and Anatolian notables around it.

Actors

Mustafa Kemal, the nationalist army, the Grand National Assembly, Greece, the Allies, and the last Ottoman sultan.

Connections

Ottoman Empire (M2), capitulations, Tanzimat, constitutionalism (M3), nationalism (M4), Kemalism and Reza Shah (this module).

Interpretations
  • Heroic founder who saved Turkish independence and modernized the country.
  • Authoritarian state-builder who ran a one-party state and suppressed Kurds, for example after the Sheikh Said rebellion of 1925, and religious opponents.
  • Continuity thesis: outside context Erik Zurcher argues Kemalist reforms continued projects begun by the Tanzimat and the Young Turks.
Today
outside context Turkey's borders are essentially those of Lausanne, and the Kurdish question that Kemal's nationalism left unresolved remains central to Turkish politics.
Model ID

Mustafa Kemal, later Ataturk, was an Ottoman officer who led the Turkish War of Independence after World War I and founded the Republic of Turkey in 1923. He organized resistance from Anatolia from 1919 against Allied partition under the Treaty of Sevres and defeated Greece in 1922. The Treaty of Lausanne in 1923 recognized Turkey's borders and ended the capitulations. He abolished the sultanate in 1922 and the caliphate in 1924, then built a one-party, secular nation-state. His rule is seen both as heroic modernization and as authoritarian state-building that continued Tanzimat and Young Turk projects and marginalized Kurds and religious opponents.

Kemalism

Thread: the modern state
What it means

The ideology of Ataturk's Republican People's Party, summarized in six principles (the Six Arrows) adopted in 1931 and written into the constitution in 1937: republicanism, nationalism, populism, statism, secularism (laiklik), and revolutionism or reformism.

Reforms
  • 1924: caliphate abolished; religious courts closed; a Directorate of Religious Affairs (Diyanet) created to control mosques and religious instruction.
  • 1925: Sufi lodges closed; the fez banned in favor of brimmed hats.
  • 1926: a civil code based on Swiss law replaced Islamic family law and banned polygamy.
  • 1928: Islam removed as the state religion; the Latin alphabet replaced Arabic script.
  • 1930s: state-led industrialization (statism) after the Depression; women gained the vote in national elections in 1934.
Secularism, precisely

Laiklik meant state control and supervision of religion, not separation. The state absorbed religious functions through the Diyanet and decided what official Islam looked like. This is secularism as differentiation of spheres directed by the state.

Why it happened

To build a unified nation-state, break the political power of religious institutions, and catch up with Europe.

Actors

Ataturk, the Republican People's Party, the army, and the bureaucracy.

Connections

Ulama (M1), Tanzimat and Osmanlilik (M3), nationalism (M4), Ataturk and Reza Shah (this module), Nasser (M6).

Interpretations
  • Modernizing revolution that made Turkey secular and Western-oriented.
  • Authoritarian imposition on a religious population, which produced a lasting cultural divide.
  • The military saw itself as guardian of Kemalism and intervened in 1960, 1971, 1980, and 1997.
Today
outside context Since 2002 Erdogan's AKP has rolled back many Kemalist secular practices, and in 2020 Hagia Sophia was reconverted from a museum to a mosque. The AKP uses the Diyanet, a Kemalist tool, to promote religion, which shows institutions outlasting the ideology that created them.
Model ID

Kemalism is the ideology of Ataturk's Republican People's Party, defined by six principles adopted in 1931: republicanism, nationalism, populism, statism, secularism, and reformism. Its reforms included abolishing the caliphate in 1924, adopting a civil code based on Swiss law in 1926, and replacing Arabic script with the Latin alphabet in 1928. Its secularism meant state control of religion through the Directorate of Religious Affairs rather than separation. Compared with Reza Shah's program in Iran, Kemalism went further because it operated in a republic and faced ulama already weakened by Ottoman reforms. Its legacy is contested in Turkey today, where the AKP uses Kemalist institutions for different ends.

Reza Shah

Thread: the modern state
What happened
  • An officer in the Persian Cossack Brigade (1878 to 1944) who took part in a coup in February 1921, outside context with encouragement from British officers.
  • He became minister of war and then prime minister in 1923. He considered a republic but dropped the idea after opposition from the ulama, who had watched Turkey abolish the caliphate.
  • The parliament deposed the Qajars in 1925, and he was crowned in 1926, founding the Pahlavi dynasty.
Reforms
  • A conscript army and forced settlement of nomadic tribes.
  • A centralized bureaucracy and secular courts and codes that reduced the ulama's legal role.
  • The Trans-Iranian Railway, completed in 1938 and financed largely by taxes on tea and sugar.
  • State factories and the University of Tehran (1934).
  • Dress laws: European-style hats for men (1928 and 1935) and a 1936 decree banning the women's veil in public, enforced by police.
  • The country's name in foreign usage changed from Persia to Iran in 1935.
Fall

His ties to Germany led Britain and the Soviet Union to invade Iran in August 1941. He abdicated in favor of his son, Mohammad Reza, and died in exile in 1944.

Actors

Reza Shah, the army, the ulama, tribal leaders, Britain, the Soviet Union, and the Anglo-Persian Oil Company.

Connections

Ulama and Sunni/Shi'i (M1), Safavid and Qajar (M2), defensive developmentalism and constitutionalism (M3), Ataturk and Kemalism (this module).

Interpretations
  • Modernizer who built the first strong central state in modern Iran.
  • Despot who ruled by force, amassed huge landholdings, and humiliated religious Iranians, especially with forced unveiling. That resentment fed the opposition that culminated in 1979.
Today
outside context The Islamic Republic's compulsory veiling mirrors Reza Shah's compulsory unveiling: both states coerced women's dress to define the nation. The 2022 protests after the death of Mahsa Amini in morality police custody challenged that coercion.
Model ID

Reza Shah was a Cossack Brigade officer who took part in a 1921 coup, ended the Qajar dynasty in 1925, and founded the Pahlavi dynasty in 1926. He built a conscript army, settled tribes by force, centralized the bureaucracy, built the Trans-Iranian Railway, and secularized courts. His 1936 ban on the women's veil was enforced by police. Like Ataturk, he built a state by decree, but he founded a monarchy rather than a republic and faced an independent Shi'i ulama that forced him to drop his republican plans. Britain and the Soviet Union forced his abdication in 1941, and resentment of his coercive secularism fed the opposition that later produced the 1979 revolution.

Synthesis

How these terms connect, 1914 to 1940s

World War I turned 19th century pressure into a new order. Britain, needing allies, made three commitments that pointed in different directions: independence to Sharif Hussein, a division of the region with France, and a Jewish national home in Palestine. The Arab Revolt was fought on the strength of the first promise.

After the war, the mandate system reconciled imperial control with the new language of self-determination. Britain and France built states in Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Palestine, and Transjordan, and the Hashemites who led the Arab Revolt received thrones in Iraq and Transjordan. The nation-state became the only legitimate unit, even where its borders were new.

Where local forces were strong enough, they built states themselves. Mustafa Kemal defeated the partition plan, won recognized borders at Lausanne, and built a secular republic. Reza Shah took power in Iran and built a centralized monarchy. Both continued the defensive developmentalism of the 19th century by decree, but Kemalism went further because the Ottoman reforms had already weakened Turkey's ulama, while Iran's Shi'i ulama remained independent.

One-sentence takeaway for essays

World War I and its settlement imposed a system of bordered nation-states on the Arab Middle East through the mandates, while Turkey and Iran built their own states by decree, and the contradictions of British wartime promises left grievances that outlasted the empires that made them.
